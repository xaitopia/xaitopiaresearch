## Part One: The Light in the Smog

### Chapter 1: The Lantern and the Rain

The power went out at seven-fifteen, which meant it had lasted a full twenty minutes longer than the night before, which Mama Nkechi said was practically a miracle.

Femi did not agree, but he had learned, over twelve years of living with his grandmother, that disagreeing with Mama Nkechi on matters of gratitude was a battle that offered no prizes. So he said nothing and reached under the bed.

The lantern lived there, on a folded piece of cloth, cushioned from the concrete floor. It was a kerosene lantern, the old kind, with a base of heavy cast brass that had gone green at the seams and warm gold everywhere else, and a glass chimney that had somehow survived everything Ajegunle had ever thrown at it. The glass was thick and slightly wavy, the way old things are, so that the flame inside it always looked like it was moving even when the air was perfectly still. Which the air in Ajegunle never was.

Femi's father had bought it in 1999 from a hardware trader in Alaba market, two years before Femi was born and three years before the malaria took him. Femi knew this because Mama Nkechi had told him, many times, in the particular way she had of telling stories about the dead: slowly, with pauses, as if the man were still nearby and might want to correct a detail.

He set the lantern on the upturned crate that served as their table and struck a match. The wick caught on the second try — it always needed two — and the flame settled into its small, steady life, and the room changed.

That was the only word for it. Changed. The lantern's light was warm in a way that the single naked bulb, on the evenings when NEPA remembered Ajegunle existed, never was. It turned the water-stained walls the colour of old honey. It softened the concrete floor and made the two sleeping mats look almost comfortable. The peeling paint above the window, which in daylight looked like a skin disease, became in the lantern-light a pattern of shadows that resembled, if you were twelve and had some imagination, the maps they drew in Geography class. The cramped, low-ceilinged room that measured ten feet by twelve and held two mats, one crate, a small cooking burner, and all of Femi and Mama Nkechi's worldly possessions became, in the brass light, something that felt almost spacious. Something that felt, against all available evidence, like a home.

Outside, the rain began.

It came the way Lagos rain always came — not gradually, not with the polite warning of distant thunder, but all at once, a full-throated assault, as if the sky had been holding it in all day and had finally given up trying. The sound on the zinc roof above them was immediate and total: a pa-pa-pa-pa-pa that started as a clatter and built in seconds to a roar, so loud that Femi could feel it in his back teeth. The corrugated iron amplified everything. Somewhere in the compound, a zinc sheet that had not been properly secured began to rattle and bang with a sound like an argument.

Mama Nkechi appeared in the doorway from the tiny side room where she kept the burner and their provisions — a small bag of garri, a tin of groundnut oil, the remains of a smoked fish wrapped in old newspaper. She had a cup of tea in each hand, which told Femi that she had been planning the power cut long before it happened, which was also typical of her.

She was a small woman, Mama Nkechi, with the particular compactness of someone whose body had spent a long life making do with what it had. Her face was deeply lined but mobile, capable of a vast range of expressions of which amusement was by far the most frequently deployed. She wore her house wrapper — the faded one, blue with a pattern of white flowers that had been washed down to suggestions — and on her head a folded cloth that she wore in the house the way other women wore their headties in church: with a firm, uncompromising neatness that announced to the world that just because you were poor did not mean you could not be presentable.

She set Femi's tea in front of him and lowered herself onto her mat with a small sound that was not quite a groan but was in the vicinity. She looked at the lantern. Something moved across her face — a softness that was not sentimentality but was close to it.

"Your father," she said, "bought that lantern because he said a man's home should have a real light. Not one that comes and goes when NEPA decides. A light you control." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "He was stubborn about things like that."

"You always say he was stubborn."

"He was. You are too." She said this without judgment, as a simple fact about the world. "The Adeyemi men are not built for bending."

The rain intensified. A gust pushed through the gap under the door and the lantern flame bowed sideways, then steadied. Femi watched it settle with the particular attention he gave to the lantern on rainy nights — the alertness of someone guarding something irreplaceable.

Mama Nkechi sipped her tea. Then, because the lantern was lit and the rain was falling and these were the conditions under which she told stories, she began.

"There was a man in our village," she said, "who had a great enemy. Another man who had taken his land and lied about it before the elders, and won. The first man was so full of rage that he spent his days thinking of nothing else. He stopped farming. He stopped greeting his children. He sat in front of his compound and sharpened a machete that he intended to use on the second man, and he sharpened it every day, and the blade grew thin, and the man grew thin, and his farm grew over with weeds, and his children grew up without a father who was actually present."

Femi knew this story. Or he knew a version of it. He drank his tea and waited.

"You know what happened to the first man?" she said.

"He got sick," Femi said.

"He got sick. He got sick from rage, which is a real sickness, not a story one. His enemy lived another twenty years and died a fat, comfortable death in his own bed. And the first man died at fifty-five with a machete that could split a hair and a farm that was worthless and children who remembered him only as a sharpening sound." She looked at Femi over the rim of her cup. "A man who cannot forgive is like a man who drinks poison and expects his enemy to die."

"I heard you the first time, Mama."

"I know. I'll keep saying it until you need to know it."

The rain thundered on the zinc. The lantern held its light. Femi did not understand, that night, why his grandmother was telling him this particular story. He was twelve years old and the world, as he understood it then, was loud and poor and difficult, but it was also predictable, and the lantern on the crate was as reliable as anything in it, and the tea was warm, and Mama Nkechi's voice was the most familiar sound he knew.

He fell asleep to the rain. He did not know it would be the last good night for a long time.

### Chapter 2: The Scavenger's Hustle

Morning in Ajegunle did not arrive. It erupted.

By five-thirty, the compound was already in full noise. In the face-me-I-face-you block where Femi and Mama Nkechi shared a room, sixteen families occupied sixteen rooms arranged in two facing rows with a narrow corridor between them — so narrow that two adults passing each other had to turn sideways, and arguments about the single bathroom at the end of the block began before the sun was fully up. Femi had learned, at an early age, to use the bathroom before four-thirty or wait until seven, when the worst of the morning rush had resolved. He chose early.

By six, the compound smelled of six different breakfasts cooking on six different burners: the sweet smoke of agege bread frying somewhere, the sharp snap of pepper soup someone had heated from the night before, the particular dense smell of pap, and underneath all of it the permanent background smell of the open gutter that ran along the back of the block — a slow, black, ammoniac stream that nobody had cleaned in living memory and that produced, in the dry season, a smell that settled into your sinuses like a tenant who refused to pay rent.

Femi ate a small bowl of garri with groundnut and two cubes of sugar, standing at the door of their room because the crate was occupied with Mama Nkechi's mending. He watched the compound wake up: children being chased into uniforms, a woman arguing with a Keke driver through the compound gate, an old man coughing with the steady, mechanical reliability of the generators that began humming from the scrap yard two streets over the moment the morning shift started. The generators never stopped. They were the permanent low note of AJ City, a bass hum beneath everything, the sound of a place that had never once been given enough.

He packed his sack — an old feed bag repurposed with a length of rope for a strap — and told Mama Nkechi he was going to the market end.

"Come back before three," she said, without looking up from her mending. Her needle moved with a speed that suggested it was doing the work independently, her hands just a platform for it. She was mending her church wrapper — the good one, the starched white and indigo that she wore on Sundays and hung to dry in the shade on Monday and folded with the precision of a cartographer on Tuesday. The wrapper was four years old and had been mended more times than Femi could count, but on Sunday mornings, Mama Nkechi wore it like it had been cut fresh from a bolt of new cloth that morning, and she walked into the church on Lagos Island with her back straight and her headtie perfect, and nobody who looked at her would have guessed that she had eaten garri for three days to afford the bus fare.

"Yes, Mama."

"And stay away from behind the scrap yard."

He said yes to this too, with the practiced ease of someone who said yes to things he intended to evaluate later.

The streets of Ajegunle in the morning were a system of controlled chaos that rewarded those who understood its rules. The Danfo buses ran their routes with an aggression that treated traffic signals as theoretical constructs — their yellow bodies pushing through intersections with a confidence that was either deeply brave or deeply ignorant, and the difference was academic because the result was identical. Their conductors hung from the open doors and shouted their destinations into the noise: Iyana-Tedo! Wharf! Mile Two! The shouting had a rhythm to it, a kind of music, though Femi would not have called it that.

He worked the route between the main market and the bus stop, which was the richest vein for cans. The soft drink and malt vendors clustered at the stop, and by mid-morning the ground around the benches and wooden tables was reliably seeded with flattened tins. Femi moved through the crowd with the particular economy of a boy who had done this many times: head down, eyes scanning the ground, the sack over his shoulder, sandals squelching in the soft red mud that the night's rain had made of the unpaved path.

The mud was deep today. It sucked at his sandals with each step — a wet, organic sound, like the earth was trying to make a decision about whether to keep them. The mud was the colour of old blood and it stained everything it touched. Femi's feet, by eight o'clock, were red to the ankles, the mud working up through the gaps in his sandals and packing between his toes. He had learned not to mind this. What he minded was the mosquitoes, which were always worse after rain — fat, slow, arrogant mosquitoes that landed on you with the confidence of creatures who know they have never once been successfully swatted.

He had collected about forty cans when he heard the whistling.

It was a two-tone whistle — short, then long — and it came from the direction of the canal path. Every child in the three streets around Ajegunle primary school knew that whistle. Femi straightened and moved, on instinct, to the side of the nearest building and pressed his back against the wall.

The Iron Scrappers moved in a loose formation of five. They were older — fourteen, fifteen, sixteen — and they occupied the path the way gangs occupy public spaces: not by standing in the middle of it, but by making everyone else move to the edges. They wore the uniform of boys who had decided the rules of ordinary life did not apply to them: the ripped jeans, the oversized shirts open at the chest, the particular expressionless faces that were a practiced performance of not caring. The one in front was Viper.

Femi had seen Viper many times. You could not live in these three streets and avoid him. He was taller than the others by half a head, and he moved with the slow, deliberate looseness of someone who has learned that speed signals desperation. His real name, according to the older women in the compound, was Emmanuel Okafor, which he would have killed you for saying aloud. He had a scar along his left jaw that he did not bother to hide because it told a story that was useful to him. He was sixteen and had the eyes of someone considerably older — not wiser, but harder, which was a different thing entirely.

At the edge of the group, half a step behind and half a step to the side, was a boy Femi did not know.

He was thinner than the others, this boy, with the particular thinness of someone who had not been eating enough rather than someone who was naturally lean. His clothes were torn at the knees and elbow, not fashionably but genuinely, and he was carrying a coil of copper wire that was visibly heavy because his shoulders were uneven under its weight. He had a face that had not yet settled into the expressionlessness of the others — Femi could see the effort it was taking, the active work of composing his features into something that said he belonged. He was watching Viper the way a dog watches the person who controls its food.

Femi had not seen this boy before. But he recognized the situation. He had seen it before, in different configurations: a boy from outside the circle, orbiting the gang, hoping that proximity to their menace would grant him some protection. It never worked the way the boy hoped. It never worked at all.

The Iron Scrappers passed without looking at Femi. He was invisible to them, which was exactly how he wanted it. He waited until the whistling faded and the group turned the corner past the mechanic's shed, then peeled himself off the wall and went back to his cans.

He had reached sixty-three by noon. Sixty-three cans, at thirty-five naira a kilo, would give him enough for one of the three textbooks he needed before September. One of three was not three. But it was one more than zero, which was where he had started, and Mama Nkechi had a word for people who did not count such things: ungrateful.

He headed home, the sack heavy on his shoulder, sandals squelching, the afternoon heat coming off the zinc rooftops in visible waves. He did not know that the boy with the copper wire had watched him leave.

### Chapter 3: The Shattering

Three days later, on a Tuesday, Mama Nkechi went to the market and Femi went with her as far as the junction and then came back.

He came back because he had forgotten his collection sack. He was certain he had hung it on the nail inside the door, and as he turned into their alley his mind was already running ahead to the can route — the bus stop, the market edge, whether the rain last night had generated a fresh deposit near the mechanic's yard. His mind was not on the room or on the door of the room, which was why it took him several seconds to process what he was seeing.

The door was open.

Not open the way a door is open when it has been left carelessly ajar by someone in a hurry. Open the way a door is open when the flimsy pin-latch that substitutes for a proper lock in rooms like theirs has been forced — the frame slightly splintered at the hasp, the door resting at an angle it should not rest at.

Femi stopped walking.

Every instinct he had acquired in twelve years of living in Ajegunle — every rule about doorways and alleys and situations that did not look right — told him to stay outside. To call out. To wait. He ignored all of them and pushed the door fully open with the flat of his hand.

The thin boy from the canal path was crouched beside the upturned crate, both hands on the lantern.

For a moment — it could not have been more than two seconds, though it had the stretched, elastic quality of shock — neither of them moved. The boy looked at Femi. Femi looked at the boy. The boy's hands were on the brass base of the lantern, his fingers wrapped around it, in the middle of an act that had no innocent interpretation.

Then the boy ran.

He made for the door. Femi was in the doorway. What happened next was not planned by either of them — it was a collision of panic and instinct, Femi lunging for the lantern, the boy wrenching away, both of them stumbling into the narrow corridor between the facing rows of rooms. Femi got a hand on the lantern's brass body and held. The boy's elbow caught him across the cheekbone — a sharp, bright pain — and Femi's grip slipped to the glass chimney.

"Let go!" the boy gasped. His voice was high, not with anger but with fear, and underneath the fear something else — a desperation so concentrated that Femi, even in that moment, felt it like a heat coming off the boy's skin.

Femi did not let go.

They lurched into the alley. The boy was stronger than he looked — he had the wild strength of someone who had decided that losing was not an option his situation allowed him. He wrenched the lantern sideways and Femi stumbled, and the boy turned, and there was a cinderblock wall at the end of the alley, and what happened next took perhaps one second.

The boy brought the lantern down against the cinderblock.

Not the whole lantern — just the brass base, which was what he wanted, which could be sold by weight. The glass chimney, which he did not want, which was just an obstacle, took the impact first. The sound it made was not a crash. It was a sharp, clear, almost musical note — a single ring, very brief, very high — and then silence, and then the tinkling of glass falling in small pieces onto the alley dirt.

The brass base had separated cleanly. The boy had clearly understood something about the lantern's construction that Femi had not — that the chimney was held to the base by a metal ring, and a sharp impact would break the ring and separate the two parts. He had prepared for this. He had come into Femi's room knowing exactly what he was going to do.

He ran. He was around the corner and gone in the time it took Femi to understand what had just happened.

Femi stood in the alley with the lantern's metal ring in his hand — it had come off with the glass — and looked at the ground.

The glass lay in perhaps a dozen pieces. The curved glass of the chimney had broken in long shards, and the smaller pieces glittered in the dirt between the larger ones, catching the afternoon light with a brightness that was almost offensive in its beauty. The lantern's glass had been old and thick and slightly wavy, and the shards showed that waviness in cross-section — you could see the quality of the thing in the way it broke, the way you can see the grain of good wood when it splinters.

Femi crouched down.

He picked up one of the larger pieces. It was cool. It had a slight smell, the way old glass has a smell — something mineral, something clean. He turned it in his fingers and it caught the light and made a small brightness on the alley wall that moved as his hand moved.

His father had bought this lantern in 1999 from a trader in Alaba market.

He set the piece of glass down in the dirt. He set down the metal ring beside it. He stayed crouched for a long time, long enough for his knees to begin to ache, long enough for the compound sounds to come back to him — someone cooking, a radio, a child crying two rooms away. Long enough for the first thing to settle into place inside his chest.

It arrived not as a feeling but as a physical sensation — a heaviness, low and central, like something dense had been placed behind his breastbone without his permission. He pressed one hand to his chest, instinctively, the way you press on a bruise to check whether it is real. It was real. It sat there and it was cold, and it was heavy, and it was the first intimation — though he did not have the words for it then — that what had been taken from him was not just glass and brass.

He was still crouched in the dirt when Mama Nkechi came home.

He heard her footsteps in the corridor and did not stand up. She came to the doorway of the alley and took in the situation — the broken glass, the boy on his haunches, the ring in the dirt — and was quiet for a long moment.

"Chinedu," she said. Not a question. She said it the way people say the names of local disasters, like they had been expecting it.

"You know him?"

"His mother lives in the next block. She works nights at the cleaning company." She stepped forward and crouched beside Femi, looking at the glass. Her face, up close, showed something that was not quite anger and not quite grief — a worn, seasoned sorrow, the sorrow of someone who has seen enough of the world's casual cruelties to have stopped being surprised but not stopped being pained by them. "He's been following those boys for weeks. The Iron Scrappers. I watched it coming."

Femi said nothing. The weight in his chest sat.

"Come inside, Femi. Don't sit in the dirt."

"Leave me."

His voice came out strange. Flat and quiet in a way that was different from his ordinary quiet, and Mama Nkechi heard the difference, and for once she did not push. She stood up, slowly, and went inside, and the sounds of her moving around the room came out to Femi in the alley — the familiar sounds of someone making order out of disorder, making do with what remained.

He stayed in the dirt until the light changed. The broken glass glittered and the weight in his chest sat and did not move, and something in Femi's understanding of the world quietly and permanently rearranged itself around the new cold fact that had taken up residence inside him.

## Part Two: The Iron Heart

### Chapter 4: The Taste of Ash

The room was dark now at night. Not dark the way it was dark when the power cut — that was a temporary dark, the kind you lit a lantern against. This was a permanent dark, a new condition, the dark of a room that had become simply the place where you were, rather than the place where you lived.

Femi lay on his mat on the first night after the shattering and stared at the ceiling he could not see. Beside him, at a careful distance, the upturned crate held a short wax candle that Mama Nkechi had produced from somewhere. The candle's light was thin and wavery and reached only to the centre of the room, leaving the corners in shadow. It smelled of cheap paraffin and cast no warmth. Femi looked at it once and looked away.

Outside, Ajegunle went about its night-business with complete indifference. The generators throbbed. Somewhere a few compounds over, a Fuji music set was playing at the volume that was standard for Lagos after nine p.m. — loud enough to be heard over the generators, over the dogs, over the rain when it came, over all competition. The bass sat in the walls of the room like a second heartbeat. Femi pressed one hand to his chest and felt his own heartbeat and the bass simultaneously, and both of them felt wrong.

He did not sleep well. He slept in the broken, shallow way of someone whose body is resting but whose mind has refused to clock out, cycling through the same images: Chinedu's hands on the lantern. The sound of the impact — that single, musical ring. The glass in the dirt. The brass base in a stranger's hand, on its way to a scrap dealer, to be weighed and paid for and melted, the history in it dissolved as thoroughly as if it had never existed.

By Wednesday, he had stopped picking cans.

It was not a decision. It was more like a withdrawal — the way a fire doesn't decide to go out, it simply runs out of what it needed to keep going. The thing that had gotten him up each morning, that had organized his days and given them a direction — the schoolbooks, the saving, the steady accumulation of cans that converted, at thirty-five naira per kilo, into something that had a future attached to it — that thing had gone quiet. The iron in his chest had replaced it.

He spent his days in the way the weight told him to spend them: sitting on the step outside the room, watching the alley. He was not watching for anything in particular. He was not watching for Chinedu, or not only for Chinedu. He was watching the way a person watches when they have been made aware that the world contains threats they had not previously accounted for, and they are trying to map them.

The mosquitoes were bad on the step. He let them land. He had stopped caring about small discomforts. The large one in his chest crowded them out.

Mama Nkechi watched him with the careful, patient attention of someone who knows that the wrong intervention at the wrong moment will close a door that is still marginally open. She brought him food — rice on Wednesday, yam on Thursday, eba with egusi on Friday — and set it beside him and did not comment when he ate three bites and set the rest down. She did not comment because she understood what was happening, even if Femi did not have the language for it yet. She had seen it before. She had lived it herself, thirty years ago, when Femi's grandfather died owing a man money he had not taken, and the man told the village he had, and she had spent six months sharpening a grief into something that hurt her more than it ever hurt him.

On Friday evening she said, very gently, "Your schoolbooks, Femi. You have until September."

"I know."

"The cans don't collect themselves."

"I know, Mama."

She looked at him. He was sitting against the wall with his knees drawn up, and in the poor evening light he looked younger than twelve — he looked like the boy he had been at eight, at six, before the weight had arrived to make him seem older than he was. "Chinedu isn't worth your future," she said.

The name went into Femi like a key into a lock. Not opening anything — just turning the mechanism, clicking the tumblers into their aligned positions. He felt the iron in his chest do something that was not quite movement but was a kind of intensification, a settling deeper into place.

"Don't say his name in this room," Femi said.

Mama Nkechi was quiet for a long time. Then she said, "Okay," and went inside, and Femi sat on the step and listened to the generators and the Fuji music and the distant, aggressive conversation of the Danfo conductors at the stop on the main road, and the weight in his chest sat and did not move.

By Saturday, the food tasted of nothing. Or not of nothing — ash was closer to it. He could taste that it was food, could identify rice as rice and yam as yam, but the flavour had been replaced by something grey and dry, as if his mouth had forgotten the point of eating. He ate enough to function and no more, and Mama Nkechi did not comment, but he saw her eyes on him when she thought he wasn't looking.

On Sunday, she put on her starched Sunday wrapper — the good one, white and indigo, ironed the night before with a charcoal iron until the starch stood out from the fabric like armour — and tied her headtie in the careful, high arrangement that she only used for church, and stood in the doorway and looked at him.

"Come with me today."

"I'm not going."

A pause. "You've not missed a Sunday in three years."

"I'm not going."

She looked at him for a long moment with an expression that held everything she wanted to say and had decided not to. Then she gathered her Bible and her purse — which contained, Femi knew, exactly enough for the bus fare there and back and the offering envelope that she would fill with the coins she had been saving from the week's provisions — and she left.

Femi sat in the dark room and listened to the sounds of Sunday in AJ City, which were marginally different from the sounds of every other day: more church music from various directions, more dressed-up people in the alley, the particular Sunday morning smell of frying akara from the woman three doors down who sold it after service. Even the Danfo conductors seemed to shout their destinations with slightly less aggression. The generators still throbbed. The gutters still smelled.

He thought about Chinedu. He thought about him not as a person but as a location — a specific point in the neighbourhood around which his anger was organized, the way a splinter is a specific point around which flesh organizes its inflammation. Every thought he had about his future, his schoolbooks, his father's lantern, eventually arrived at Chinedu the way all the gutters in Ajegunle eventually arrived at the black canal.

The iron in his chest was heavier today than it had been on Wednesday. He pressed both hands to his sternum and felt its weight, that cold, dense heaviness that was not physical pain but was close enough to it that the difference was academic.

He did not yet understand that he was the one drinking the poison.

### Chapter 5: The Gang's Illusion

On the afternoon Femi was sitting on his step drinking the poison, Chinedu was carrying scrap metal.

The metal was a section of copper pipe — industrial, heavy, salvaged from somewhere Viper had declined to specify — and it had been cut roughly, so that both ends were jagged in the way of metal that has been worked with impatience rather than craft. Chinedu had it across both shoulders, his arms hooked over it in the manner of a yoke, and he was walking behind Viper and two other Iron Scrappers along the canal path toward the dealer's yard on Wharf Road.

The copper was heavy. It pressed down on his shoulders with the flat authority of something that did not care about the skin beneath it, and the jagged ends, which extended past his shoulders on both sides, caught on walls and on the doorframes of the compounds they passed and required constant small adjustments that scraped the raw edges across his collarbone each time. His hands, at the ends of the yoke, had small cuts from earlier in the week — shallow ones, but in the heat and the mud of Ajegunle, shallow cuts had a way of staying interesting longer than they should.

Viper was talking about something. He was often talking about something — a supplier, a plan, a person who had made a mistake and was going to understand the consequences of that mistake soon. He talked the way people talk when they have learned that their voice produces a physical effect on people around them — not because of what it says, but because of what it might say. Chinedu had spent two weeks studying this and had not yet stopped being afraid of it.

He had given Viper the brass on the day he took it.

He had stood in the yard behind the scrap dealer's compound and held out the brass base of Femi's lantern — still warm from Femi's room, still carrying the faint smell of kerosene — and Viper had taken it without ceremony and handed it to one of the others who weighed it on a small scale and announced a number. Viper had nodded. The money — a fold of notes that Chinedu had not been allowed to count — had gone into Viper's pocket. Chinedu had waited for some acknowledgment that he had done what was asked, had paid what was owed, was now something he had not been before.

Viper had clapped him once on the shoulder and said, "Now you owe me commission."

Chinedu had not known what commission meant in this context. He had learned, over the following days, that it meant he carried things. He carried wire and pipe and scrap iron from the places the Iron Scrappers collected it to the dealer's yard on Wharf Road. He was not paid for this. It was not presented as an exchange — it was presented as the natural order of things, the duty owed by the newest and least to the more established, which was the arrangement in all such hierarchies whether they called themselves gangs or families or businesses.

Nobody explained this to him. He was simply expected to understand it.

What Chinedu had expected, when he began his weeks of orbiting the Iron Scrappers, was something that he would have been ashamed to name clearly: safety. Not money, not status, not the excitement that the gang's movements suggested from a distance. Safety. He was thirteen and fatherless and living with a mother he saw for three waking hours a day because she worked nights, and the streets of Ajegunle had a grammar of threat that he had been reading alone for two years. He had seen what happened to boys who had no protection — the casual extractions, the beatings from other boys, the sense of moving through the neighbourhood as a permanent target. He had believed, watching Viper's group from the outside, that belonging to it would convert him from target to threat.

What he had not understood — what none of the boys who orbited the Iron Scrappers seemed to understand until they were inside it — was that the hierarchy did not offer protection to its lowest members. It offered them only the privilege of being exploited by the people above them rather than by strangers. This was, Chinedu was beginning to understand, not a meaningful improvement.

"You're slow," one of the other boys said, without turning around. His name was Tunde and he was fifteen and he had been carrying things for Viper for six months and had, in consequence, adopted Viper's manner of speaking about people as if they were objects whose relevant properties were their weight and their function.

Chinedu adjusted the pipe on his shoulders and said nothing. The jagged end scraped his collarbone. He kept walking.

The dealer's yard smelled of rust and the particular metallic sweetness of cut copper and the underlying chemical smell of the solvent they used to strip wire. It was full of the compressed noise of industrial work: the clang of metal on metal, the whine of a grinder somewhere in the back, the raised voices of men in constant negotiation. Chinedu set his load down in the designated area and straightened his back slowly, pressing both hands to his spine in a gesture that was decades too old for his body.

Viper conducted the transaction. Chinedu watched him — the ease of him, the way he spoke to the dealer with the flat confidence of someone who knows the price of everything and will pay none of it. The dealer was older, fatter, and had presumably more experience of the world, and yet he moved around Viper with a careful deference that Chinedu had noticed before and still did not fully understand. Power was not always where age put it.

On the way back, Chinedu fell into step beside one of the quieter members of the group — a boy named Emeka who was sixteen and had been with the Iron Scrappers for almost a year and who, unlike the others, occasionally acknowledged Chinedu's existence without using it as an opportunity to establish the relevant hierarchy.

"How long," Chinedu said quietly, "before I don't carry things anymore?"

Emeka looked at him sideways. Something moved in the older boy's face — not pity exactly, but recognition, the look of someone seeing in another person an earlier version of a mistake he is still paying for. He looked away. "You keep asking that," he said, "and you'll be carrying things until you're too big to be useful."

"Then what?"

Emeka didn't answer for a moment. The canal path was wet from last night's rain, the black water running slightly higher than usual, carrying its cargo of plastic bottles and foam cups and unidentifiable dark matter with unhurried efficiency. The smell came off it in waves — the ammonia sharp enough to make your eyes water, and underneath it something biological and deeply wrong.

"Then he finds someone else to carry things," Emeka said finally. "And you become the one who explains to the new boy how it works."

Chinedu walked in silence for the rest of the path. He thought about the lantern. Not about the brass — the brass was gone, dissolved into the dealer's inventory, into Viper's pocket, into the anonymous metallic future of all scrap. He thought about the glass. He had not expected the glass. He had seen the lantern as a brass object with a glass obstacle, and he had dealt with the obstacle quickly and without ceremony, and the sound it made — that single clear note, that musical ring — had been so clean, so brief, so unlike the sound of damage, that he had almost convinced himself it was not damage at all.

He had heard Femi fall to his knees.

He had not looked back. He had been running and he had not looked back, but he had heard the sound — not a cry, not a shout, but a single, wordless sound that a person makes when something has been taken that was not just a thing. Chinedu knew that sound because he had made it himself, the day two years ago when his father's bag had finally been collected from the room by his father's uncle, taken to wherever it was going, the last physical evidence of the man dissolved into someone else's life. He knew the sound. He had made himself unhear it.

He was thirteen years old and he had made a choice that he could not unmake, and the weight of it was settling, slowly, in a place not far from where the weight in Femi's chest had settled — and neither of them knew yet that those two weights were, in fact, two halves of the same thing.

### Chapter 6: The Encounter at the Canal

By the second week, Femi started leaving the room again.

Not to pick cans. Not for school or church or any of the purposeful reasons that had once organized his days into something that moved forward. He left because the room, in the dark, had become too small for the iron in his chest. The weight needed space. It needed to move. So Femi moved, and the weight moved with him, and together they walked the streets of Ajegunle the way a man walks after bad news — not going anywhere, but unable to be still.

He knew every alley in this neighbourhood. He had mapped them, over twelve years, the way a scavenger maps a territory: not for beauty, not for history, but for what each path offered and what each path cost. He knew which gutters flooded first in the rain, which compound gates were left unlocked, which bends in the alleys offered cover and which were traps. He knew the canal path the way he knew his own hands.

The Ajegunle canal was not the kind of canal that appears in geography textbooks. It was a wide, slow, permanently black channel that ran along the southern edge of the neighbourhood and into which everything that the gutters carried eventually deposited itself. The water did not move so much as it accumulated — layers of dark matter compressing over time into something that was less water than memory, a liquid archive of everything AJ City had discarded. The smell was its own weather system. On dry days it merely stung. After rain it became physical, something you could feel on your skin, a dampness with intentions. The reeds along the bank were not green but a dull, petroleum grey, and they grew thick enough that you could not see the water from the path unless you walked to the very edge.

Femi was on the canal path on a Wednesday morning, ten days after the

shattering, not thinking about anything in particular and thinking about Chinedu constantly, which was the condition he had been living in. His sandals were dry today — the first dry day in a week — and the path was cracked red clay that sent up small puffs of dust with each step. The generators hummed. A kite circled overhead, patient and enormous, riding a thermal above the zinc rooftops.

He saw Chinedu before Chinedu saw him.

The boy was crouched at the base of the canal bank, thirty yards ahead, doing something with a piece of wire. He had his back to the path, his attention on whatever he was working at, and he had not heard Femi's approach over the noise of the neighbourhood. He was alone — no Iron Scrappers, no Viper, no performance of belonging. Just a thin boy in torn clothes crouched over wire in the dirt, and the canal running its slow, dark business behind him.

Femi stopped.

The iron in his chest did something it had not done in ten days. It moved. Not lighter — heavier, but in motion, like something that had been frozen in place suddenly remembering it had a direction. His hands found the path without him deciding anything. The first stone was there, half-buried in the cracked clay, the right size, the right weight — the kind of weight that a twelve-year-old hand understands instinctively, the weight that means reach.

He picked it up.

He turned it in his hand and felt its edges and looked at Chinedu's back thirty yards away. The boy had not moved. The wire was still occupying his hands. The canal ran behind him, black and slow and entirely indifferent.

Femi raised his arm.

The thought was clean and clear in a way that thoughts are sometimes clean and clear when we have stopped pretending to ourselves that we are better than we are: he

smashed my father's lantern. He took the only thing I had. He plunged my room into darkness and took the brass to a scrap dealer and in thirty years there will be nothing left of my father in this world, because the only thing left was glass and brass and now there is only broken glass in an alley. He deserves this. He deserves more than this.

The stone was warm from the clay. His arm was raised. Thirty yards.

Then Mama Nkechi's voice spoke in the interior of his skull with the precise intonation she used for things she had said so many times they were no longer sentences but objects: a man who cannot forgive is like a man who drinks poison.

Femi's arm came down.

Not slowly — quickly, with the sudden exhausted motion of a person who has been holding something very heavy for too long and simply stops. He opened his hand and the stone fell onto the path and he spat into the clay beside it, a single sharp movement, because the rage had to go somewhere and spitting was all he had.

He walked away. He did not run. He walked with the deliberate, rigid gait of someone maintaining composure through sheer structural will, and he did not look back, and Chinedu remained crouched at the canal bank, still working his wire, never knowing that Femi had been there.

He walked three streets before he stopped, in the narrow gap between a mechanic's shed and a compound wall, and put both hands on the zinc and leaned his weight against it and waited for his breathing to steady.

He had dropped the stone.

He had done the right thing. He had dropped the stone and walked away from Chinedu and spared him whatever injury the stone would have done, and he had done this because Mama Nkechi's voice had arrived in time, and it should have felt like something. Goodness should register. He had been taught, in twelve years of Sunday

school and lantern-lit evenings and his grandmother's patient instruction, that good choices produce a particular sensation — a lightening, a clarity, the feeling that things are in their right order.

He pressed both hands flat against his chest.

The iron was still there. Exactly as heavy as it had been when he picked up the stone. It had not shifted. It had not thinned. He had chosen correctly and the poison was still in him and the lantern was still broken and the room was still dark, and the dropping of the stone had not, it turned out, changed any of the conditions that had produced the stone in the first place.

This was the thing nobody told you about forgiveness. It was not a moment. It was not a single correct choice at the canal bank that ended the matter and restored everything to its right order. Dropping the stone was not the same thing as putting down the weight. They were connected, these two actions — one of them was a necessary beginning of the other — but they were not the same thing, and he had confused them, and now he stood in the gap between the mechanic's shed and the compound wall with his hands on his chest and understood, with a clarity that was almost physical, that the work was not done.

He stayed in the gap until the iron settled back to its resting weight. Then he pushed off the wall and went home, and the afternoon closed over AJ City like a zinc lid, and the generators throbbed their endless bass note, and nothing had changed and something had begun.

### Chapter 7: The Betrayal

The storm that came on Saturday was the kind that Lagos produces three or four times a year — not a storm that builds and announces itself, but one that arrives fully assembled, as if it had been waiting just outside the city limits for permission to enter and had finally lost patience.

The sky went from white to purple in twenty minutes. The temperature dropped five degrees in the time it takes to walk from the main road to the canal path, and every generator in Ajegunle seemed to feel it and cough simultaneously. The wind came first — a hot, dry blast that flipped market awnings and sent plastic bags spiraling up above the zinc rooftops in lazy arcs — and then, with the specific abruptness of Lagos rain at its most decisive, the water arrived.

Pa-pa-pa-pa-pa.

The sound on the zinc roofs was different in a real storm from the sound in an ordinary downpour. An ordinary downpour sounded like applause. A real storm sounded like judgment — the zinc amplifying every drop into its own small percussion, ten thousand of them per second, until the sound was no longer a collection of individual impacts but a continuous wall of noise that made conversation impossible and thought effortful. The gutters, which in dry weather barely moved, became within minutes deep, fast, brown channels, carrying their cargo of red mud and plastic and whatever had been left in the streets with absolute indifference to its value.

Femi was inside when it started. He sat on his mat and listened to the roof and felt the iron in his chest and watched the water begin to find its way under the door — a thin line of it, red-brown, feeling its way across the concrete floor toward the mats with the quiet persistence of water that has learned it will eventually get where it is

going. He rolled the mats up onto the crate, which was the established emergency procedure, and he waited.

He did not know, sitting dry in the dark room while the storm rearranged the streets of Ajegunle, that three blocks away on the canal path, Chinedu was learning the final lesson the Iron Scrappers had for him.

They had been moving a load of copper wire from a storage point near the mechanic's yard to the dealer on Wharf Road, hurrying because the sky had made its intentions clear. Viper was in front, moving with the particular confidence of someone who believes that weather, like most things, will make an exception for him. The others followed. Chinedu was last, as always, carrying the largest coil of wire over both shoulders.

The wire was heavy and the path was already running with an inch of water by the time they reached the canal stretch. The path beside the canal was the worst section in rain — the clay turned immediately to a slick, treacherous mud that offered no grip, and the edges of the path dropped six feet into the canal water below with nothing between them. Chinedu moved slowly and carefully, which was the correct way to move on this path in this rain, and which fell significantly behind the pace Viper had set.

He never knew, afterward, exactly what caused it. A shift in his weight. A section of path edge that had been more undermined by the water than it appeared. His right foot went out and his body tilted and his arms went wide for balance and the coil of copper wire came off his shoulders.

It fell into the running gutter beside the path with a sound he would hear in his sleep for months: a thick, wet splash, followed by the particular rushing sound of something valuable being carried away in fast water.

He watched it go. The wire tumbled once in the current and disappeared around the corner of the canal bank in two seconds, three seconds, gone.

Viper turned around.

Chinedu had been afraid of Viper before. He had felt that fear in various registers over the three weeks he had been trying to belong to the group — the low-grade fear of disappointing him, the sharper fear of drawing his direct attention, the fear that lived behind every instruction given too quietly and every look held a second too long. But this was different. The thing that moved across Viper's face now, standing in the rain on the canal path, was not the controlled threat that he deployed as a management tool. It was something less managed and more primary, and Chinedu understood in the specific, body-deep way that animals understand territorial signals that what was coming was not going to be symbolic.

"Run," said Emeka, under his breath, beside Chinedu. "Run, man."

Chinedu did not run. His legs had made a decision to stay planted and his mind had not been consulted, and by the time the consultation was complete Viper was already close.

What happened next took perhaps four minutes. Chinedu had experienced pain before — the grinding poverty of Ajegunle distributed it generously and without application — but this was organised, intentional, and witnessed, which made it a different category of pain entirely. Two of the boys held his arms. Viper was efficient about it, the way a person is efficient about things they have done before. The rain came down and washed the blood off the path as fast as it appeared.

When it was over, Chinedu was on the ground in the alley behind the compound wall, which was where they had moved him because the canal path was visible from the road. His shoes — the ones he had been wearing when he had broken into Femi's room, the ones with the hole in the left sole that he had been stuffing with cardboard for three months — had been taken off his feet and thrown into the gutter. He watched them go with a detached, floating attention that his mind had adopted as a protective measure.

Viper crouched down to his level. The rain was hammering the zinc above them in a continuous roar. Viper's voice came through it with perfect clarity.

"If I see your face in these streets again," Viper said, in the measured tone of someone reciting a policy rather than making a threat, "I will put you in the canal. You understand? Not as a threat. As a fact."

He stood up. The Iron Scrappers left.

Chinedu lay in the alley and the rain fell and the storm shook the zinc rooftops and the gutters ran brown-white with the rush of water, and the alley smelled of mud and rot and the old, deep smell of garbage that had been there through many rainy seasons and would be there through many more. He tried to sit up once and his ribs explained why that was a poor idea, so he lay back and let the rain fall on his face and stared up at the narrow strip of purple-black sky between the compound walls.

He was cold. His feet were bare on the wet ground and the cold came up from the concrete through his ankles and settled in his legs, which were the least painful part of him right now. The mud seeped through the back of his shirt.

He thought about his mother, who would be sleeping now before her night shift, who had not known he was involved with the Iron Scrappers, who believed him when he said he spent his days at his friend Ebuka's house. He thought about what she would say when she saw him. He thought about what she had already lost — his father, the clean life she was building around his absence, the careful fiction that things were going to be all right if she just kept working the night shift and he just kept staying out of trouble.

He had not stayed out of trouble. He had bought himself into trouble with brass that wasn't his, and now the trouble had taken his shoes and left him in a garbage alley in a storm.

He thought about the sound the lantern had made.

He thought about it the way you think about a thing you have tried very hard not to think about — all at once, completely, the way it breaks through when the pain makes the usual defenses impossible to maintain. The glass breaking. The sound of Femi on the ground. The warmth of the brass in his hand as he ran. The brass that now lived in a scrap dealer's yard, sorted by weight, stripped of its history, waiting to be melted.

"I'm sorry," he said, to nobody. To the strip of sky. To the rain. The words tasted strange in his mouth, the way words taste when you have been swallowing them for too long.

The alley did not answer. The storm did not care. The gutters ran and the zinc rattled and the generators, heroically and pointlessly, kept on.

## Part Three: The Grace of the Gutter

### Chapter 8: The Boy in the Mud

Mama Nkechi sent Femi out at six.

Not into the full storm — the worst of it had moved east by then, leaving behind the steady, determined secondary rain that Lagos produces after a major downpour, as if to make sure the message has been received. The streets were running. The gutters were carrying their brown water at speed. But it was possible to move through it, if you didn't mind your feet.

"We need akara for tonight," Mama Nkechi said, pressing a fifty-naira note into his hand. "Mama Seun will still be frying. She fries in any weather, that woman."

"Send me tomorrow," Femi said. He did not want to go out in the rain.

"I'm sending you now." She looked at him with the expression that preceded all conversations about getting up and getting on with it. "The rain won't drown you, Femi. You are not made of sugar."

He went.

The streets after a Lagos storm had a particular quality that was not quite beauty but was close to it if you were not focused on the practical problem of getting anywhere without wading. The red mud ran in all directions, finding every low point, pooling in the depressions that marked the places where someone's cooking fire had been, the places where the path dipped, the places the earth knew from experience. The air smelled different — cleaner, the ammonia of the gutters temporarily diluted, the burning tire smell washed out, the deep red-earth smell of wet laterite rising up to replace everything. For perhaps thirty minutes after a big storm, Ajegunle smelled like it could have been anywhere.

Femi walked with his sandals in his hand. The sandals were pointless in this — the straps would just fill with mud and the soles were thin enough that he could feel the ground through them anyway, and barefoot at least he had control of what he was stepping in. The mud came up warm between his toes. The secondary rain fell on his hair and shoulders in a fine, insistent curtain.

He was three streets from Mama Seun's frying station, taking the shortcut through the alley behind the Celestial Church compound wall, when he heard it.

It was not a cry. A cry would have been easier to place, easier to attribute, easier to walk past on the grounds that other people's crises were their own business and the akara would not stay hot. It was lower than a cry — a sound from below the level of communication, from the place where the body speaks before the voice has decided to. A low moan, a hitch of breath, the sound of a person whose resources have run out and who is making sounds not to signal but simply because the body continues its processes regardless.

Femi stopped.

He stood in the rain in the alley with the fifty-naira note curled in his fist and listened. The sound came from the left, from the gap between the church wall and the next compound — a narrow space, not an alley exactly, more an oversight in the architecture, the kind of gap that AJ City produced wherever buildings had been assembled without coordination. It was full of the secondary rain and old garbage and the smell of things that had been wet many times.

He turned left.

He almost did not recognize Chinedu at first. The boy was face-up in the mud, his back against the compound wall, his legs extended. He was soaked through entirely — shirt, trousers, skin — and the mud had found all the horizontal surfaces and settled there: in the hollows of his collarbones, on his forearms, along the sides of his neck. His face had a quality of stillness that was not peace but its opposite — the stillness of

a person who has stopped having the energy to be otherwise.

His feet were bare. His face was cut at the cheekbone and the lip. There was mud on the cuts so that distinguishing the blood from the general redness was difficult.

Femi looked at him.

The iron in his chest did something complex and immediate. It did not melt — nothing so clean as that. It shifted, and in shifting it revealed something that had been beneath it this whole time, something he had not had access to while the iron was sitting on top of it. What it revealed was a feeling he recognized, though he had not felt it in these circumstances: satisfaction. Dark, cold, complete satisfaction. The particular fulfilment of seeing a debt settled.

This is justice.

The thought came without guilt, which made it more honest. Chinedu had taken something irreplaceable from him and plunged him into a darkness that had lasted two weeks and had no certain end, and now Chinedu was in a darkness of his own making in a garbage-filled gap in the rain, and the streets of Ajegunle — which dealt consequences with a thoroughness that no magistrate could match — had done what Femi's stone at the canal had not done. The world, on its own schedule, had been just.

Femi turned around.

He took two full steps back toward the alley. The rain fell on his bare shoulders. Mama Seun's akara was warm and the fifty naira was in his hand and the storm was fading and the night was coming and the room at home had a wax candle on the crate and Mama Nkechi would be waiting.

He took a third step.

He thought, on the third step, of Chinedu's bare feet. He did not mean to think about them. He was thinking about the akara, was actively trying to think about the akara, and instead he thought about the bare feet extending into the mud, and what it

meant in Ajegunle to not have shoes, and how cold the mud was coming up between his own toes right now, and what it would feel like to be lying in it rather than walking through it.

He stopped himself from thinking about it.

He took a fourth step.

On the fourth step he thought about the moan. The way it had sat below the level of communication, from the place before language, from the place where the body simply reports its conditions without asking for permission from the person it belongs to. He knew that sound. He had made something close to it himself, in the alley, with the broken glass in his hands.

He stood still in the rain on his fourth step. He was not yet turning back. He was just standing still, which was different from going back, which was different from making any decision at all.

He was twelve years old and barefoot in the mud and the iron was in his chest and the boy who had put it there was in the gap in the wall and the night was coming to Ajegunle the way it always came: all at once, completely, without asking anyone's permission.

### Chapter 9: The Heavy Lift

The thunder came from behind him.

Not the thunder that precedes rain — that was already done, already spent, the storm's announcement long dissolved into its own delivery. This was the thunder that a Lagos storm sometimes produces on its way out, after the main body of it has passed east, a retrograde percussion, as if the storm is having second thoughts. It was a single massive crack, very close, that split the remaining drizzle and shook the zinc rooftops and sent a loose sheet on the compound above him clanging and rattling in its fastenings.

Femi flinched.

Not from fear, or not only. The sound hit something inside him, something that was not thought but was adjacent to thought, and what it shook loose was not a memory so much as a sensation: the sound of the glass. That single, clear, musical note. The sound the lantern had made when Chinedu brought it down against the cinderblock — not a crash but a ring, high and brief and final.

He stood in the rain on his fourth step and the echo of the thunder faded into the zinc rooftops and the generators and the endless ambient noise of the neighbourhood, and what remained after it was a question he had been avoiding for two weeks:

Who are you going to be?

Not the question his grandmother had asked him, not the question his Sunday school teacher had asked, not the question in the verse they quoted at him. His own question, in his own voice, from the part of him that had been watching the other parts manage the iron and had been quiet until now. Who are you going to be, Femi? The boy on the step, sharpening his grief into a point? The boy with the stone at the

canal? Or something else? Something that costs more than both of those and produces something the other two cannot?

He thought about the man in his grandmother's story. The man with the machete and the overgrown farm and the children who remembered him only as a sharpening sound. The man who had chosen the satisfaction of his rage over every other thing in his life and had ended up with only the rage, which was the one thing in the inventory he would have been better off without.

He thought about the way his father's lantern had turned their ten-by-twelve room into something that felt spacious. Not because of what it was made of — not the brass, not the glass, not the kerosene — but because of the quality of the light it made. The warmth of it. The way it touched the peeling walls and the water stains and the concrete floor and made all of them into something else. Light did not change what it shone on. It changed how you saw it.

He turned around.

It was not a dramatic turning. He did not feel a warmth spread through his chest or a weight lift from his shoulders. He felt precisely the same as he had felt on the fourth step — iron in place, rain on his head, mud between his toes — except that now he was facing the direction of the gap in the wall instead of facing away from it, and his feet were moving, and the distance between him and Chinedu was decreasing instead of growing.

The groan of frustration that came out of him as he walked back was real and unsuppressed. It was the sound of someone doing a thing they do not want to do, have not talked themselves into wanting to do, and are doing anyway, which is a different and more costly variety of doing a thing than the kind where you have first arranged your feelings into the correct configuration. He was not glad to be doing this. He was doing it mad.

He crouched down beside Chinedu in the mud.

Up close, the boy looked worse. The cuts on his face had mixed with the rain and the mud into a dark, reddish paste that covered most of the right side of his face. His breathing was shallow and quick, and Femi could hear something in it — a careful quality, the breathing of someone who has discovered that deep breaths produce a specific pain and has accordingly adjusted. Ribs, Femi thought, with the pragmatic assessment of a boy raised in a neighbourhood that produced regular object lessons in human damage.

"Chinedu."

The boy's eyes opened. They found Femi's face, and in the moment of recognition — the exact second when Chinedu understood who was crouching over him in the mud in the gap in the wall — something happened in those eyes that Femi had not expected and could not look away from. It was not relief. It was not gratitude. It was, for one completely unguarded second, pure terror. The terror of a person who has done harm and who understands, in the specific way that guilt understands such things, that the harmed person being this close in these conditions is possibly the worst situation possible.

The terror lasted one second. Then Chinedu closed his eyes again.

"I'm going to sit you up," Femi said. His voice came out flat and functional, stripped of everything except the information it needed to carry. "Don't try to help. You'll make the ribs worse. Just let me move you."

He was not large for twelve. Neither was Chinedu particularly large for thirteen. But a person who has been beaten and is cold and is lying in mud has a particular kind of weight — not the clean, cooperative weight of someone who is helping you — but the resistant, settling weight of a body that has decided, at some level below consciousness, to remain horizontal. Femi got both hands under Chinedu's armpits and pulled, and the mud relinquished him with a sucking reluctance, and the sound of it was the sound of the earth itself registering an opinion.

Chinedu gasped when he came vertical. His hand went to his side, pressing there, and his jaw set, and he breathed in a sequence of very small, very controlled increments that told Femi everything about the ribs without requiring a word.

"Can you walk?"

A pause. A tiny, miserable nod.

"We're going to Mama Nkechi's. It's six minutes. Hold my shoulder."

Chinedu's hand found Femi's shoulder. His grip was weak — cold fingers, no real strength in them, the grip of someone who is holding on because holding on is the only instruction their body has managed to retain. Femi took his weight and they moved into the alley.

Six minutes, normally. Twice that tonight, because the gutters had overflowed onto the path in several places, and some of those places were knee-deep with the fast brown water that you could not step into without checking first where the drop was, and because every time they crossed uneven ground Chinedu's breathing changed in a way that meant slowing down. They moved through the flooded streets of Ajegunle in the secondary rain, two boys in the dark, one supporting the other, both of them soaked to their bones, neither of them speaking.

The iron in Femi's chest had not gone. He felt it still — the weight, the density, the cold fact of it sitting behind his breastbone. But something had changed around it, some quality of the surrounding material, as if the iron were sitting in different conditions than it had been sitting in for two weeks. It was the same weight. The territory it occupied had altered.

At the corner of their block, Femi shifted Chinedu's weight more fully onto his own shoulder. Chinedu made a sound — not a word — and Femi tightened his grip and said, "Almost," because it was true and because it was the only useful thing he had.

The compound gate was unlocked. He pushed it open with his foot.

The light from under their door was the thin yellow of the wax candle, which was the light that their room had now, which was the light of a room without a lantern. Femi looked at that thin yellow line under the door and felt the iron do its complex, shifting thing, and then he raised his free hand and knocked.

The door opened immediately, which meant Mama Nkechi had been awake and listening for him. She took in the situation in one sweeping look — Femi, Chinedu, the mud, the rain, the careful way Chinedu was holding his side — and her face did something complicated and quick. Femi watched her make a decision and put it away and replace it with the expression of a woman who has work to do.

She stepped back and opened the door wider and said, "Come inside out of the rain."

She did not ask who. She did not ask why. She did not ask what had happened or whose fault it was or what it meant that her grandson had arrived at her door in a storm carrying the boy who had broken his father's lantern. She simply stepped back and opened the door and let them in, and to Femi, standing in the doorway with his enemy's arm across his shoulders and the rain still falling and the iron still in his chest, those four words were the most extraordinary thing he had heard in two weeks.

Come inside out of the rain.

As if it were simple. As if the door of a twelve-year-old's house in Ajegunle were the kind of door that opened without conditions. As if grace were just the name for what you did when the weather was bad and a person needed shelter.

Maybe, Femi thought, stepping through the door, that was exactly what it was.

### Chapter 10: The New Light

Mama Nkechi boiled water.

This was the first thing she did, and she did it with the particular concentration of a woman who has learned that when a situation is too large to address all at once, you address the next immediate thing. The water boiled on the small burner while she cleared the second mat and laid it out, and while the water heated she produced from the bottom of her tin provisions box a small bar of carbolic soap and a clean cloth that had once been part of a wrapper and had been cut up and saved because nothing was thrown away in this room that had any further use in it.

Chinedu had collapsed onto the mat without assistance. His body had simply deposited itself there, making a quiet decision on behalf of his legs, which were done. He lay on his back and stared at the ceiling, breathing in the small, cautious increments he had been using since the canal path.

Femi sat on his own mat and wrung the water out of the hem of his shirt and watched his grandmother work.

She was not gentle the way people are gentle in films — not tender and whispering and moving with soft hands through soft light. She was efficient, which was the Ajegunle version of gentleness, the kind that comes from a life in which sentiment is a luxury you use sparingly but practical care is nonnegotiable. She cleaned the cuts on Chinedu's face with the warm water and the carbolic soap and Chinedu winced at the carbolic — everyone winced at carbolic, it was the obligation of the soap — and Mama Nkechi said, without pausing, "Still. You've had worse tonight than carbolic, I think."

Chinedu was still.

She checked his ribs with the focused, systematic touch of a woman who had raised children in places where doctors were not the first resort. She pressed along the ribcage and watched his face and said "here?" and "here?" and when she reached the place where the answer was yes, she nodded and said, "Bruised, not broken. You'll feel them for two weeks." She wrapped him with a strip of cloth she tore from the same former wrapper — not a medical wrap, but firm, supportive, the kind that tells the ribs to stay where they are and behave. Then she produced from somewhere her own good blanket — the one she kept folded at the bottom of her mat, the thick one, the green one with the pattern of yellow diamonds — and laid it over him.

Femi watched her lay the green blanket over Chinedu.

He had slept under that blanket himself, every cold-season night for as long as he could remember. It smelled of Mama Nkechi — of the particular combination of coconut oil and camphor that she used on her skin and on her fabrics and that meant, to Femi, the smell of being taken care of. She laid it over the boy who had broken his father's lantern with the same hands and the same expression she had used every cold-season night of Femi's life, and Femi sat on his mat with his wet shirt in his hand and felt something shift in his chest that was large and slow and not yet complete.

She gave Chinedu water to drink, tilting the cup because he couldn't sit up fully. She gave Femi the akara she had made herself from what was in the provisions — not the Mama Seun akara, which Femi had never reached, which was fine, which didn't matter now — and she sat down on her mat and drank her tea and said nothing, and the three of them occupied the small room in the light of the wax candle, and the rain fell outside, and the generators throbbed, and the night settled over Ajegunle in its ancient, unremarkable way.

Femi ate the akara. It was the first thing he had tasted — actually tasted — in two weeks. The bean cake was hot and faintly sweet and spiced with the particular pepper combination that Mama Nkechi used, and it was not extraordinary akara, it was

ordinary akara made quickly from what was available, but it did not taste of ash. He ate and felt the warmth of the food move through him and said nothing.

Chinedu slept.

He went under quickly and deeply, the sleep of a body that has been given shelter and warmth and has immediately delegated all remaining decisions to the unconscious parts. His breathing evened out, losing the careful, rationed quality it had carried all evening. Mama Nkechi watched him sleep for a moment with an expression that was entirely her own — complex and private and not available for interpretation.

Then she looked at Femi.

She did not say: you did the right thing. She did not say: God is pleased with you. She did not say: this is what I have been teaching you. She said nothing. She held his gaze for a long moment with the look she used for things that didn't require words because words were not the right instrument for them. Then she got up, slowly, with the small sound that was not quite a groan, and she folded her own wrapper and laid it at the end of her mat and went to sleep.

Femi sat in the wax candle light and listened to both of them sleep.

He was awake for a long time. The iron in his chest was still there — not gone, not magically dissolved by the act of crossing the four steps in the alley and turning back. It was still there and it still weighed what it weighed, and he understood now, with the clear-eyed understanding of exhaustion, that it was not going to be resolved tonight. He had carried a broken boy through the rain. That was not the end of anything. It was, at most, a beginning.

He was still awake when Chinedu woke up.

It was somewhere past midnight. The rain had stopped entirely. The generators were still running, as they always were, but the neighbourhood had reached the

particular quality of quiet that it achieved only in the deep hours, when even Ajegunle had used up its daytime energy and settled into something like rest. The wax candle had burned down to a stub, its flame very small, casting its thin circle of light that barely reached the walls.

Chinedu opened his eyes and found the ceiling and then turned his head and found Femi sitting on the mat across from him.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

Chinedu looked at the ceiling again. He looked at the wax candle. He looked at the green blanket over him. He looked at the room — the peeling walls, the upturned crate, the absence where the lantern would have been. He looked at it for a long time, and Femi watched him look at it, and understood what Chinedu was understanding.

When Chinedu looked back at him, his face had none of the arrangements on it that people put on their faces in ordinary circumstances — no dignity, no composure, no careful management of how he appeared. Whatever had been maintaining those arrangements had been used up somewhere on the canal path. What was left was a thirteen-year-old boy in an unfamiliar room, under a borrowed blanket, looking at the person he had wronged.

"Femi."

His voice came out rough and low.

"I know," Femi said.

"I need to—"

"I know."

"No." Chinedu shifted, trying to push himself up, and the ribs stopped him. He pressed a hand to his side and breathed his small breaths and tried again, slower. He managed to get his back against the wall. "No. I need to say it. Let me say it."

Femi was quiet.

Chinedu looked at the place on the crate where the lantern would have been. He looked at it for long enough that the looking was itself a kind of speech.

"It was your father's," he said. "I knew. Everybody in the block knows what that lantern is." He stopped. Something moved across his face, the precursor of something larger. "I told myself I was desperate. I told myself I needed the protection more than you needed the lantern. I told myself a hundred things that all said the same thing, which was that my need was bigger than your — than what you—" He stopped again. His jaw was working. "I heard you fall. When I ran, I heard you in the alley. I heard what it sounded like when you understood what I'd done." He was crying now, not loudly, not with the theatrical generosity of performed tears, but the quiet, inconvenient way that people cry when they are actually sorry — a tightening, a leaking, the face trying to maintain itself and failing. "And I kept running. I kept running for ten days and told myself I was surviving. And the thing I was running from wasn't Viper or your grandmother or you. It was that sound. That sound you made in the alley."

He pressed the back of his hand to his face.

"I'm sorry, Femi. I'm asking you — I'm asking, not expecting — I'm sorry."

The room was very quiet. The candle stub was nearly out. In its last small light, the peeling paint on the walls had taken on a quality that Femi remembered — the honey-coloured warmth that belonged to the lantern, or something close to it, the way the right light changes not what it shines on but how you see it.

Femi looked at Chinedu for a long time. He looked at the bare feet extending beyond the green blanket — bare, because the shoes had gone into the gutter on the canal path. He looked at the cuts on his face and the careful way he was holding his side and the wet clothes that Mama Nkechi had not had a replacement for and had left him in because in a room this small you make do. He looked at all of it and understood something he had not understood before tonight: that the iron in his

chest and the weight that Chinedu had been carrying were not separate debts. They were the same damage, moving through two different people, and neither of them would be well until it was set down.

He breathed in. He breathed out.

"I forgive you," he said.

The words came out plain and undecorated, the way true things come out when you have stopped managing them. Not magnanimous, not theatrical, not the voice of someone who has conquered their feelings and arranged them into the correct configuration. The voice of a twelve-year-old boy, tired and wet, sitting in the dark in his grandmother's room, saying a thing that was true and that cost him something real to say.

And the iron moved.

Not all at once — not the sudden dramatic dissolution he might have expected from the stories. It moved the way something frozen thaws: from the edges inward, slowly, the borders of it losing their definition, the cold density at the centre becoming something less absolute. He pressed his hand to his chest and felt it — felt the edges of the thing, which had been hard and certain for two weeks, becoming less certain, less bordered, the weight redistributing itself into something that was still present but was no longer fixed.

He could breathe deeper than he had in two weeks. That was the first thing he noticed. A single deep breath, filling both lungs properly for the first time since the alley, and the air tasted of carbolic soap and wax candle smoke and the particular damp smell of a room after rain, and all of it tasted like itself and not like ash.

Chinedu was still against the wall. He reached into the pocket of his trousers — the mud-caked trousers, the ones he had been wearing all day — and produced a small, damp fold of naira notes. He set it on the mat between them. In the last thin light of the candle stub, Femi could see that it was a small amount. Not enough for a

brass lantern — not within sight of enough for a brass lantern. Perhaps enough for three or four months of can-collecting to make up the difference. Perhaps the beginning of the beginning.

"It's what I have," Chinedu said. "I can't fix what I broke. I know that. But it's what I have right now, and I want you to have it."

Femi looked at the fold of naira notes in the candlelight.

He thought about what it meant to begin. Not to fix — to begin. To take the first step of the long work of making something right, knowing the full step count is not yet visible, knowing the destination is further than today's effort can reach. His grandmother began this way every Sunday — the pressed wrapper, the counted coins for the offering, the bus fare that left nothing to spare. His father had begun this way in 1999 in Alaba market, buying a brass lantern because he believed a home should have a real light, and in the buying of it had started a story that was still going, still warm, still producing light in ways that had nothing to do with kerosene.

He picked up the fold of naira notes.

"Thank you," he said.

The candle stub went out.

In the dark, Femi became aware of the room without light — the particular quality of an Ajegunle night when all the candles are out and the clouds are covering the moon and the only illumination is the remote orange suggestion of the street, filtering through the gap under the door. It was a dark he had lived in for two weeks and had believed was his permanent condition. It was the dark of the room without a lantern.

But it was different tonight. The dark was the same dark — the same dimensions, the same walls, the same zinc above rattling in the last of the night wind. But he was different in it. The iron in his chest was in motion, thawing from its borders inward,

and Mama Nkechi was breathing in her steady sleep on the other mat, and Chinedu was in the room that had been dark for two weeks because of him, and the naira notes were in Femi's hand.

He thought about what his father had said, through Mama Nkechi, in every story she had told about him: a man's home should have a real light. Not one that comes and goes. A light you control.

The lantern had been glass and brass and kerosene. Those things could be broken, melted, burned. They had been. But what the lantern had been made of — the intention behind it, the warmth it had produced, the way it had turned a ten-by-twelve room into a place where a boy and his grandmother told stories and drank tea and the peeling paint looked like palace walls — that was not made of brass. That was made of something that did not have weight and could not be carried to a scrap dealer and could not be measured on a scale. It was made of the choice to produce warmth in the dark, which was a choice that could be made with a brass lantern or a wax candle or no light at all, made only with the will to keep being a particular kind of person regardless of what the night brought.

That light could not be smashed.

He lay back on his mat in the dark and listened to the last of the night wind finding the zinc, and the generators throbbing their eternal bass note, and Mama Nkechi and Chinedu breathing in their different sleeps, and the distant sound of a Danfo bus on the main road, making its aggressive way toward somewhere, the conductor's voice cutting through the dark: Mile Two! Mile Two!

Femi closed his eyes.

He breathed — fully, deeply, both lungs engaged — and felt the iron continue its slow thaw, and thought about candle-light and brass-light and the light that cannot be made of any material at all, and sleep came to him the way it comes when a burden has been shifted: not all at once, not dramatically, but thoroughly, and with

something that felt, against all the available evidence of an Ajegunle night, almost like peace.

Outside, the generators ran. The gutters drained. The stars appeared, one by one, through the clearing clouds above the zinc rooftops of AJ City.

And in a ten-by-twelve room, in the dark, three people slept.
