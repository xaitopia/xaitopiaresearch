## Chapter 1: The Indigo Stain

The mist came before everything else.

It arrived each morning the same way — not gradually, not with the polite announcement of weather that gives you time to prepare, but all at once, as if the mountain had exhaled in its sleep and the breath had simply stayed. By five o'clock the ancient village at the summit of Idanre was inside the cloud entirely, and the world below — the iron-roofed town, the tarred road that curved away toward Akure, the cars that winked in distant sunlight like dropped coins — ceased to exist. Up here there was only the mist, the smell of wet red laterite, and the slow, insistent sound of Baba Odun's loom.

*Clack-clack-swoosh.*

Temi had been hearing that sound since before he understood what it was. As a very small child he had believed it was the sound the mountain made when it was breathing — that the great hills, which his grandmother had told him were the oldest things in Ondo, drew their breath in the clack and let it out in the swoosh, and that the weaving was simply how the mountain stayed alive. He was eighteen now and understood the mechanics: the heddles separating the warp threads, the shuttle carrying the weft across, the beater pressing each new row home. He understood it, and the understanding had not made it any less relentless.

"Your hands, Temi. Watch your hands."

Baba Odun's voice came from the other side of the compound, where the old man sat at his own loom with his back to the sunrise and his face turned always — always — toward the cloth. His grandfather's hands were a text that Temi had been reading his entire life without fully understanding. They were the hands of a man who had spent sixty years at the loom: the knuckles swollen and permanently curved, the palms calloused into something that was closer to the bark of the iroko tree than to ordinary skin. The fingers had their own grammar — they moved with a precision and economy that made every gesture look like a decision rather than a reflex. And across all of them, inescapable, deep in the whorls of his fingertips and in the creases of his palms, was the indigo. Not a dye you could wash out — a residency, as permanent as the veins beneath the skin, so that Baba Odun's hands in certain lights looked less like hands and more like the evening sky above the hills: streaked blue and navy and, at the edges, the particular deep violet that indigo turns when it has been boiled long enough.

Temi looked at his own hands.

They were the hands of a boy who did not yet know what they were for. The palms were smooth where they should have been calloused, the skin unwritten, unblemished by any commitment long enough to leave a mark. There was a smear of indigo on the inside of his right index finger where he had touched the dye pot rim yesterday, and he had spent twenty minutes this morning trying to wash it off. It had not washed off. It sat there like an accusation.

He was supposed to be threading the loom for the next section of the white cloth. The cloth had been in progress for eleven days — a single yard of Aso-Oke, traditional handwoven fabric, pure white, the kind commissioned for a chief's funeral wear in a town two hills over. It was the finest grade of work: the warp threads counted out to four hundred, the pattern a classical Idanre geometry that Temi had been learning since he was ten, and beneath all of it, invisible unless you knew what to look for, the hidden structural thread that Baba Odun called the Thread of Truth.

"Come here," Baba Odun said.

Temi went. His grandfather did not look up from his own work as Temi approached — the old man's eyes stayed on the cloth, tracking the progress of the weft with the attention of someone for whom the difference between a correctly placed thread and an incorrectly placed one was not an aesthetic judgment but a moral one. He reached out one gnarled hand and took Temi's wrist, turning his arm so that the pale, unfinished palm faced upward.

"Do you know what is wrong with your hands?"

Temi said nothing. He was not good at saying nothing — his jaw always tightened, his tongue pushed against the backs of his top teeth, his throat produced a small, suppressed sound that was not quite a word. He managed all of this without speaking.

"Nothing is wrong with them," Baba Odun said. He released Temi's wrist and returned his attention to his own loom. "That is what is wrong with them. A hand without a mark is a hand without a history. You are eighteen and your hands have said nothing yet."

"Maybe I don't want my hands to look like yours," Temi said. The words came out before the better part of his nature could intercept them, arriving in the morning air with the flat honesty of something that has been thought too many times to stay hidden.

Baba Odun's shuttle paused for one full second — the clack without the swoosh, a silence in the rhythm that was more eloquent than the rhythm itself. Then it resumed.

"Thread the loom, Temi."

Temi went back to his station. He threaded the loom with hands that were too fast and too careless, picking up threads and counting them with a speed that his grandfather would have called impatience and that Temi called efficiency. Outside, the mist was beginning to lift from the summit — the world below was reemerging from its morning disappearance, and with it the distance between up here and down there was becoming visible again. He could see the town. He could see, if he narrowed his eyes against the light, the road to Akure. He could see, if he thought hard about it, everything that lay beyond the road.

He picked up the wooden weaving shuttle.

It was a beautiful object, as functional things sometimes are when they have been made by someone who understood that function and beauty are not opposites. The wood was dark and smooth, worn to a polish by forty years of his grandfather's hands before Temi had ever touched it. It was weighted perfectly — heavy enough to carry across the loom with purpose, light enough to be precise. Temi had learned to love the weight of it, which was perhaps the only thing about the weaving he had genuinely loved, and he was holding this knowledge tightly this morning because the rest of what he was feeling was something he could not afford to look at directly.

The white cloth lay half-finished on the loom. It was extraordinary work, even Temi could see that — the geometry of the pattern was exact, the tension of the warp even across all four hundred threads, and beneath it, invisible, the Thread of Truth: a single continuous binding thread that ran the length of the underside in an intricate lattice, invisible to any buyer, invisible to any admirer, seen only by the weaver and by God.

"Baba," he said.

"Thread."

"Baba, when will a customer see the Thread of Truth? When does anyone pay for the thing no one can see?"

The loom on the other side of the compound went quiet. For a long moment the only sound was the mist dripping from the eaves, and the cough of a generator somewhere in the town far below, and the distant metallic cry of a hawk riding the thermals above the valley.

"Every time they wash the cloth," Baba Odun said, "and it holds. Every time they pass it to their children, and the children pass it to their grandchildren. Every day it does not tear when it should tear. That is when they pay for it." A pause. "The truth has a long invoice, Temi. But it always collects."

Temi looked at the half-finished cloth. He looked at the shuttle in his hand. He looked at the white warp threads stretching their four hundred rows from the cloth beam to the heddles, waiting.

He set the shuttle down in the red dirt at his feet.

He set it down deliberately, without dropping it — a controlled act, not a tantrum, which made it worse somehow, because it was clearly a choice. He stood up from the weaving stool, and the stool rocked back on its uneven legs and settled, and the loom stood waiting with its four hundred threads like an audience that has been asked to hold its applause.

"There is no future in these old stones," Temi said.

He did not say it loudly. He said it quietly and precisely, the way you say things you have been rehearsing in the back of your mind for longer than you are willing to admit. He said it to the half-finished cloth, to the shuttle in the dirt, to the mountain mist and the red laterite and the sixty-year indigo on his grandfather's hands.

Behind him, the old man's loom did not stop.

*Clack-clack-swoosh. Clack-clack-swoosh*

As if Baba Odun had heard the words and answered them not with language but with the only argument he had ever trusted.

## Chapter 2: The Brass Promise

Mr. Coker arrived on a Thursday in a car that was too large for the road.

The road that wound down from the foot of the Idanre Hills to the modern town was not designed for the kind of vehicle in which Mr. Coker traveled. It was a red laterite road, narrow, ridged by old tyre tracks and the seasonal erosion of the rains, and it accommodated trucks and ageing Toyota pickups and the occasional Peugeot with the resigned patience of a road that knew its limitations. Mr. Coker's car was a black Toyota Land Cruiser with tinted windows and a chrome grille that caught the sun and flung it back at you like an insult. It came down the road at a speed that sent the red dust billowing in all directions and parked in the centre of the town square with the casual certainty of a vehicle that did not expect to be asked to move.

Temi saw it from the top of the steps.

He had been sitting on the twenty-third step since midday — not going down, not going up, occupying the uncomfortable middle ground that had become his habitual position in recent weeks, physically manifesting the argument he was having with himself. From here he could see the town below and the village above with equal clarity, which was both the point and the problem. His back was to the summit. The shuttle was still in the dirt where he had left it.

He watched the car. He watched the man who emerged from it.

Mr. Coker was not a large man but he occupied space with the confidence of one. He wore a pale grey suit in linen — entirely inappropriate for a red-dirt road in midday heat, which told you immediately that he had people who dealt with the consequences of his choices — and shoes so polished they seemed to generate their own light. He walked into the town square and his hands were the first thing Temi clocked from the steps: they swung at his sides with a smooth, manicured looseness, the hands of a man who had not lifted anything heavier than a fountain pen in some years. No callouses. No history. The nails were clean and even, and on his right hand a gold ring caught the light the way his car's chrome caught it — deliberately, aggressively, as if announcing an alliance between the man and his possessions.

He was speaking to the cloth sellers near the market. Temi descended.

He could not have explained, afterward, what made him go down the steps that day instead of continuing to sit on the twenty-third step in his customary indecision. Perhaps it was the car. Perhaps it was the shoes. Perhaps it was the particular quality of the man's laughter when he found something amusing — a sound that carried up the steps like the smell of money, which in Temi's experience had a specific fragrance, warm and slightly dangerous.

He was at the edge of the square before he had decided to be there.

Mr. Coker saw him with the efficiency of a man who had spent a career identifying useful things. His eyes traveled over Temi once — a quick, thorough inventory — and whatever the inventory revealed satisfied him, because he smiled and extended his right hand.

"You're from the weaving families up there," he said. It was not a question. "I can tell from the hands."

Temi looked at his hands. The indigo smear on his index finger had not faded. He resisted the urge to hide it behind his thigh.

"My grandfather is Baba Odun," he said.

Something moved in Mr. Coker's eyes — a flash of recognition and then something more calculated. "Odun's grandson." He said it slowly, as if the words had weight he was distributing carefully. "I've been looking for someone with your blood. You people understand cloth the way other people understand their own names. That's worth something in Lagos."

He presented his card. The card was thick, creamy stock with raised lettering: APEX TEXTILES LIMITED. MR. GABRIEL Coker, DIRECTOR OF ACQUISITIONS. Below this, a Lagos address and a phone number that began with the prefix that meant Lekki.

He spoke for twenty minutes. Temi listened.

The offer was this: Apex Textiles was expanding its premium traditional line. They had the machines, the orders, the clientele — festivals, government events, the luxury market that had recently discovered Nigerian heritage fabric. What they lacked was someone who could be the bridge between the machine's capacity and the authentic pattern. Someone who understood the geometry of Aso-Oke not as an algorithm but in their hands. He would be paid a monthly salary that he named, and Temi heard the number and felt something happen in the back of his throat — a swallowing reflex, involuntary, the physical response to a figure that was larger than anything his grandfather earned in a quarter.

"A room," Mr. Coker said. "In my house on the mainland. Three meals. And a suit, to start — because a man should look like what he intends to become."

Temi looked at the man's shoes.

That evening, he climbed back to the summit and told Baba Odun.

His grandfather was at the loom when he came in. He was working by the light of a kerosene lamp now — the mist had closed in again and the summit was in a darkness that was its own kind of world, close and intimate, smelling of wet stone and indigo and the bitter smoke of the burning wick. The loom's rhythm did not change when Temi entered. It did not change when Temi spoke. It changed only once, when Temi named the figure he would be paid.

One beat of silence. Then the rhythm resumed.

"A cloth woven in a hurry," Baba Odun said at last, "is just a rag waiting to tear."

"He has machines, Baba. One machine does in a day what takes us a week."

"And produces in a day what takes a week to fall apart."

"You don't know that."

"I know the Thread of Truth." His grandfather turned, then, for the first time, from the loom. His face in the lamp-light was made of the same material as the hill — weathered, ancient, containing more history than expression. He looked at Temi with eyes that had been looking at cloth for sixty years and had learned to see beneath the surface of everything. "Do they have a Thread of Truth in Lagos, Temi? When you program the machine, who weaves the part that no one sees?"

Temi opened his mouth and closed it. He thought of the salary. He thought of the shoes.

"The machine will do what I tell it," he said.

Baba Odun turned back to his loom. The lamp threw his shadow large against the wall, and in the shadow his gnarled hands were enormous, moving across the cloth with their impossible grace.

"Then make sure," his grandfather said, very quietly, "that you tell it the truth."

Temi packed that night. Not much — a change of clothes, the small Bible his mother had given him before she went to Abuja to work, a phone that was three years old and held its charge for approximately six hours if you didn't ask anything of it. He stood for a moment in the doorway between his room and the compound, looking at the loom and the half-finished white cloth and the shuttle still lying in the red dirt where he had set it down.

He did not pick up the shuttle. He told himself he would finish the cloth when he returned, which was the kind of thing you tell yourself when you are not yet willing to examine what you have decided.

He left before sunrise, while the mist was still in the village and the mountain was still inside the cloud. He did not say goodbye to Baba Odun. He could hear the old man breathing in his room — the slow, even breath of someone whose sleep was as steady as his work — and he stood outside the door for perhaps thirty seconds with his hand raised to knock.

He lowered his hand.

He walked down through the mist, and the red dirt of the path was cool under his sandals, and somewhere above him a night bird called once and was silent, and the ancient village settled into the cloud as if it had never been interrupted.

## Chapter 3: The Six Hundred Steps Down

The steps had been cut into the rock face of Idanre by hands whose names had been lost in centuries. They were not uniform — each step was a different height and a different width from the one before it, some shallow as a thought, some deep as a commitment, all of them worn smooth in the centre where a thousand years of feet had found the same line. The steps did not ask your opinion of them. They were what they were, and you descended them at the pace they allowed, and the mountain watched.

Temi was not descending at the pace they allowed.

He was moving fast, his bag over one shoulder, his sandals — old leather ones, worn to the shape of his feet by three years of these same steps — finding each drop with the automatic confidence of someone who has climbed this path so many times it lives in the body rather than the mind. His hands were almost never on the rope guide. He did not need it on the way down; he needed it on the way up, where the incline and the altitude and the combined weight of everything you were carrying upward — food, water, firewood, the invisible freight of your obligations — made the rope something you were grateful for.

He was past the two-hundredth step and moving faster still when he heard her.

Not saw — heard. A specific sound, below the general sounds of the mountain morning: a gasp, a shuffle of feet on rock, the sharp knock of something dense against stone. He looked up from the step in front of him.

She was perhaps seventy. Perhaps older — the women who climbed these steps regularly had a way of becoming ageless, as if the mountain had extracted some tax on time in exchange for the strength required to live here. She wore a blue wrapper and a headtie that had come slightly loose on the left side, and on her head she was carrying a basin of yams — not a small basin, not a manageable load, but a full enamel basin, deep and round, piled high with the brown-skinned tubers, the whole assembly balanced on a folded cloth ring with the expertise of decades. She was going up. She had three hundred steps ahead of her, at minimum, and she had stumbled. Not fallen — she had caught herself on the rope with one hand and the yams had shifted but not spilled, and she was standing now on the step, breathing, her legs trembling with the fine, continuous vibration of muscles at the edge of their capacity.

She did not look up at Temi. She was looking at the step above her, measuring it, having the particular private negotiation that the body holds with the mind when neither is fully winning.

Temi checked his pace. He did not stop it entirely — he slowed, which was a different thing, the speed of a person who is aware of an obstacle rather than the speed of a person who has decided to engage with it. He moved to the right side of the steps, the narrower side, the side where you had to turn your body slightly sideways to pass.

"Mama, make we go," he said. "The city dey wait for nobody."

He laughed as he said it. Not a cruel laugh — a dismissive one, the laugh of someone who has oriented their attention so completely toward what is ahead of them that what is beside them has become scenery. He passed her. He felt, in passing, the warmth of her exertion, and he smelled the yams — that clean, starchy smell — and then both were behind him and the steps continued downward and the town was growing below him and the road to Akure was becoming more specific in the morning light.

He did not look back.

He should not have laughed. He knew this even as he was doing it, the way you sometimes know a thing and do it anyway because the knowing and the doing are separated by a distance your character has not yet learned to bridge. Something registered in the lower part of his chest — not guilt, not yet, because guilt requires time and he was moving too fast for time — but a small, specific discomfort, like a thread pulled slightly too tight, that he noted and moved past.

By the three-hundredth step the town was close enough that he could hear it: the generators, the voices, the distant pulse of a radio. By the four-hundredth step he could smell the exhaust of morning traffic. By the five-hundredth step, his sandals were hitting the lower rock — the steps here were cut more shallowly, the rock smoother, the ancient meeting the road-level world in a gradual negotiation — and by the six-hundredth he was on flat ground, red dust under his feet, the mountain behind him and the junction ahead.

He stopped and turned once. The steps rose above him into the mist, which had not yet lifted at the summit, so that the top of the ascent disappeared into white. He could not see the old woman anymore. He could not see the village. He could see approximately the first fifty steps and then the mountain swallowed the rest.

He turned back to the road. Mr. Coker's car was waiting at the junction — not Mr. Coker himself, but a driver who nodded without speaking and opened the rear door. The interior smelled of leather and something cool and chemical, the smell of controlled air in a world that had decided it did not need to smell like itself.

Temi got in. The door closed. The mountain disappeared in the rearview mirror as the car moved down the road, and the steps were already becoming memory, and the old woman was somewhere above him carrying her yams, and the Thread of Truth was sitting in the dirt beside the loom where the shuttle had rolled when he set it down.

## Chapter 4: The Highway to the Sun

The landscape changed the way a person changes when they stop being careful: gradually at first, then all at once.

For the first hour, the road from Akure still had trees — the deep green of Ondo's forest on both sides, the canopy high enough that the road felt like a corridor through something living. Temi watched it from the back seat with his forehead against the cold glass. The harmattan had thinned the trees at the tops, and where they were thinnest you could see the sky, which was the particular washed-out blue of the dry season — not the dramatic deep blue of wet season mornings over Idanre, but a pale, open blue that felt unlimited.

He was wearing his sandals. They were good sandals, leather, resoled once at the market cobbler near the base of the steps. His feet knew them completely — every stiffness in the leather, every place where the strap had been reinforced with new thread, the slight give on the right side where the buckle had been bent back into shape after a fall on the three-hundred-and-forty-second step. They were, like his grandfather's hands, a text written by experience.

By the second hour, the trees had thinned and the forest had become farmland, and the farmland had become more sporadic, interrupted by villages that grew denser and louder and more provisional as the road moved toward the city. The driver didn't speak. Temi didn't speak. The car radio was off and the only sound was the air conditioning and the road.

By the third hour, the highway had begun.

Temi had never been on the Lagos Ibadan Expressway before, and the first sight of it — the width of it, the volume of traffic on it, the sheer aggression of the vehicles sharing it — produced a feeling he was not expecting. Not excitement. Something closer to the feeling you get at the edge of the Idanre summit when you look down the face of the rock and the drop is real and your body recognizes the drop before your mind has caught up. A cold note, high and brief, somewhere behind his sternum.

Then the traffic swallowed them. The driver became different in traffic — his loose, silent quality disappeared and he drove with the focused ferocity of a man who understood that in Lagos traffic, courtesy was a liability. He changed lanes without signaling and held the horn with a confidence that suggested the horn was a primary instrument of navigation. Temi gripped the door handle with his left hand and said nothing.

They entered the city through Berger, which was not an entrance so much as an absorption. Lagos did not have edges — it had zones of increasing intensity, rings of noise and density that thickened as you moved inward, so that you did not so much arrive as become progressively overwhelmed. The smell was the first thing: the exhaust of ten thousand vehicles in gridlock, the sharp chemical smell of the drainage channels running black beside the road, the occasional sweet, frying-oil smell from a food stall on the roadside that appeared and vanished before you could identify it. The sound was the second thing: the horn was not an instrument of communication here, it was a climate, a permanent atmospheric condition, a collective voice of a city that had decided it had too many feelings to keep to itself.

Temi pressed his forehead against the glass again.

The sandals were on his feet. He looked at them. He thought about what Mr. Coker had said: a man should look like what he intends to become. He thought about the driver's shoes — the ones visible at the bottom of the driver's khakis, plain leather loafers, practical and clean. He thought about Mr. Coker's shoes, which had caught the sun in the town square and thrown it back like the car's chrome grille.

At Ojota they passed a roadside market — stalls of goods spread out on both sides of the road, traders calling, vehicles passing close enough to merchandise to read the labels. On one of the stalls, a man was selling shoes. City shoes. The kind with the thin, hard soles and the pointed toes that Temi had seen in photographs.

The discomfort in his chest — the thread pulled too tight that he had carried since the steps — shifted. Not lighter. Further down, settling below his ribs into his stomach, where it mixed with the hunger of the car journey and became a single unlocated feeling that he turned away from.

On the outskirts of Oshodi, they stopped briefly in a go-slow that did not move for eleven minutes. During those eleven minutes, Temi made a decision.

He took off his sandals. Both of them. He held them in his lap for a moment — the leather warm from his feet, the shape of his heel and arch pressed permanently into the insoles, three years of mountain steps recorded in the worn leather. Then he wound down the car window three inches — the driver noticed but said nothing — and pushed them through the gap.

They hit the road behind the car and were gone before the car moved again.

His feet were bare on the cool mat of the car floor. He did not look back. He told himself he felt lighter.

The feeling in his stomach said otherwise.

## Chapter 5: The Synthetic Soul

The suit did not fit.

This was the first thing Temi understood about his new life: that it had been made for someone else and he was wearing it anyway. The jacket was correct at the shoulders but generous at the waist, so that when he stood still it draped well and when he moved it shifted, reminding him with each movement that the interior dimensions of the garment were not his. The trousers were half an inch too long, folding slightly over the top of the new shoes, which were the correct size but stiff, the leather so new it had not yet decided to accommodate the specific architecture of his foot.

He wore the suit on the first morning and looked at himself in the mirror of the room Mr. Coker had arranged in the house on the mainland and tried to understand the person looking back. He looked older. He looked like a version of himself that had been put through a process — refined, the way ore is refined, which involves high heat and the removal of everything that isn't the target element. He straightened the jacket and turned away from the mirror and went to work.

Apex Textiles occupied a building in Oshodi that was neither large nor small — a mid-range industrial space, the kind that is built for function and then gradually modified by function until the building has the particular character of a place that does one thing very intensively. The corrugated zinc walls had been patched in several places with newer zinc that hadn't yet rusted to match the old. The floor was concrete, stained in patterns that recorded the spills and drips of a decade of production. And the looms — the power looms, the machines — were the heart of it.

They were the opposite of Baba Odun's loom the way a fist is the opposite of an open hand.

Where the traditional loom was silent between its own sounds — where the *clack-clack-swoosh* existed in relationship to quiet, so that each beat of the rhythm was defined partly by what preceded and followed it — the power looms were continuous, total, a sound that occupied all available space and left none for anything else. The whir of the motors, the screech of the high-speed shuttles, the bang of the beaters pressing each row home — not one at a time, not as a rhythm, but simultaneously, a dozen machines running at once, the sounds overlapping into a wall of noise that you felt in your teeth and in the small bones of your inner ear. After an hour, Temi stopped trying to identify individual sounds and simply existed inside the noise, which was like drowning except that it happened slowly and was unrecognised by the people around you.

He was not weaving. That was the second thing he understood.

He was an operator. He sat at a console and entered parameters — pattern codes, thread counts, colour sequences — and the machine did the rest. He had been given a manual that explained the interface, and Mr. Coker had sent a young man named Chukwu to show him the basics, and within three days Temi could operate the machine competently. Within a week he could operate it well. His hands learned the keyboard and the interface panel with the same speed they had learned the loom controls, because his hands were good hands that learned quickly, but the learning felt different. At the loom, each correct movement produced something — you could feel the cloth building, feel the warp holding, feel the particular satisfaction of the beater pressing a row home exactly right. At the console, each correct entry produced a readout confirming the entry, and the machine did something you could not feel.

He ate well. Mr. Coker's household was run by a woman named Madam Yinka who cooked the way she did everything — competently, efficiently, without sentiment. Three meals a day, on schedule, protein at two of them, the kind of eating that keeps a body functional and produces no memories. Temi ate and did not taste much and told himself he was grateful.

His hands, after two weeks, were clean in a way they had never been clean at home. No indigo. No red dirt under the nails. No callouses from the shuttle. The skin of his palms had softened — the early, tentative roughening that came from three months of more serious work at the loom had receded, like a language you stop speaking.

He pressed his palm flat on the surface of the console one morning and looked at it. Smooth. Empty. A hand that had said nothing yet, that was building no history, that would leave no readable mark on the things it touched. He thought, briefly, of his grandfather's words.

He closed his hand and entered the next parameter.

At the end of the first month Mr. Coker gave him his salary in an envelope. The amount was as promised — the number he had heard in the town square and swallowed involuntarily. Temi held the envelope on the bus back to the mainland and did not open it until he was in his room with the door closed, and when he opened it and counted the notes he felt the money's warmth for perhaps thirty seconds.

Then he put it under the mattress and lay on his back and looked at the ceiling and tried to understand why the number, which had seemed luminous at a distance, had gone so ordinary so quickly in his hand.

The machine sounds were still in his ears.

Whir-screech-bang. Whir-screech-bang.

He lay there and listened to the city through the window — the generators, the horns, the voices — and underneath all of it, coming from nowhere and everywhere, he thought he could hear the faint echo of a different rhythm.

*Clack-clack-swoosh. Clack-clack-swoosh*.

He pressed both palms to his ears to stop it and it did not stop.

## Chapter 6: The Compromise

The order came on a Tuesday morning in the third month.

Mr. Coker called Temi into his office — a room that was unlike the factory floor the way the inside of a well is unlike the surface of the ground: cool, quiet, carpeted, smelling of the leather of the furniture and the particular cologne that Mr. Coker wore, which was warm and expensive and slightly synthetic, the smell of wealth that has been manufactured rather than earned. There were framed photographs on the walls: Mr. Coker at government functions, Mr. Coker at product launches, Mr. Coker beside a cabinet minister at what appeared to be a foundation ceremony. In all of them his hands were visible — soft, manicured, ringed — and in all of them they were doing the same thing: gesturing outward, as if offering something, when what they were actually doing, Temi would understand later, was measuring what they could take.

"Sit," Mr. Coker said.

Temi sat. The chair was leather, the kind that accepts your weight and does not push back, and he felt it accepting him now with a comfort that was almost suspicious.

Mr. Coker placed a document on the desk. It was a contract — a new one, separate from the employment contract. At the top was the name of a national arts festival, a major government event, the kind that happened every two years and for which cultural fabric was ordered in the thousands of yards. Below the name was a specification: Aso-Oke, traditional pattern, premium grade. The quantity was a number that Temi had to read twice.

"The Idanre geometry," Mr. Coker said. "Your grandfather's pattern."

Temi looked at the document.

"You can programme it," Mr. Coker said. "You know the pattern better than anyone in this building. Better than me, better than Chukwu. You were born in it. And this order —" he tapped the quantity figure "— this is what makes the year. Not just for me. For you. The bonus on this contract is —"

He named a figure.

The figure settled in the room like a stone dropped in still water, and the ripples of it moved outward and touched everything — the leather chair, the framed photographs, the thin remaining distance between what Temi had and what he had come to Lagos to find.

He said nothing. He was trying to read the document and listen to Mr. Coker simultaneously, which was not possible because Mr. Coker's voice required the same attention that the document required and there was not enough of him to give to both.

"There's one modification," Mr. Coker said.

Here it was. Temi had been waiting for it without knowing he was waiting, the way you wait for the wrong note in a song that is otherwise correct — you cannot predict it but when it comes you recognize it immediately.

"The Thread of Truth." Mr. Coker said the name with the precise, careful respect of a man who does not believe in the thing he is naming but understands that other people do, which is a more sophisticated and more dangerous kind of disbelief. "The binding structure on the underside. The structural lattice." He met Temi's eyes. "Remove it from the programme."

The room was very quiet.

"Without it," Temi said slowly, "the cloth will — after a few washes, the warp will —"

"Look beautiful at the festival," Mr. Coker said. "At a government festival, the cloth is photographed, worn once, stored. It is not washed thirty times. It is not passed to grandchildren." He smiled with the portion of his face assigned to smiling. "By the time anyone notices the structure is compromised, the fabric will be three seasons old and the contract will be long settled."

He slid the contract across the desk.

"The bonus," he said, "is for the modification. For understanding that the market doesn't pay for what it cannot see."

Temi looked at the contract. He looked at the bonus figure. He looked at Mr. Coker's hands, resting on the desk on either side of the document — smooth, clean, the gold ring winking under the office light. He looked at his own hands in his lap — cleaner than they had been in Idanre, emptier than they had ever been, the indigo stain on his index finger finally, entirely gone.

He thought about Baba Odun's voice in the lamplight: The truth has a long invoice. But it always collects.

He thought about the salary envelope under the mattress. He thought about the suit that almost fit, the shoes that were still stiff, the room that had no memory of him because he had given it none. He thought about the factory floor, the whir-screech-bang, the hands that had not said anything in three months.

He thought about the Golden Fleece. That was what he had called it, in the privacy of his ambition, on the twenty-third step — the thing that was somewhere in the city, bright and achievable, that would convert his potential into something real and visible. He had come down from the mountain to find it. Perhaps this was it. Perhaps the fleece was never comfortable to touch.

He picked up the pen.

His hand was steady. He would think about this later — that his hand was steady, that there was no trembling, no physical resistance, that his body did not stage the protest that his understanding should have staged. He signed his name and the name looked ordinary on the paper, just letters, just a word, just a boy from Idanre writing himself into a document that was about to make him into something he had not set out to become.

He slid the contract back across the desk.

Mr. Coker's hand covered it. The soft fingers, the manicured nails, the gold ring. "Good man," he said, with the warmth of someone who means something slightly different by those two words than the person hearing them understands.

Temi walked back to the factory floor. The machines were running. He sat at his console and entered the programme for the festival order — the Idanre geometry, his grandfather's pattern, four hundred warp threads, the ancient design that had been passed through generations. He entered it line by line, and it was correct, and it was beautiful, and at the section that called for the Thread of Truth's structural lattice, he looked at the screen for a long time.

Then he deleted it.

The sourness arrived the moment the key was pressed. It was not a metaphor and it was not a literary device — it was a physical sensation, low and immediate, in the exact centre of his stomach, as if he had swallowed something that his body recognised as wrong before his mind had finished the argument. He swallowed against it. He pressed his hands flat on the console.

The machines ran. The cloth began to build. From the outside it was beautiful.

## Chapter 7: The Ripping

The bonus arrived three weeks after the festival order shipped.

Temi held the new envelope and looked at the number on the cheque and waited for it to feel the way it had felt when Mr. Coker named it across the desk. It did not. Money at a distance was luminous; money in the hand was just paper. He folded the cheque and put it in his jacket pocket and went out to Balogun market, because he needed new shoes — the stiff city shoes had broken down at last, the right sole separating at the toe, which happened to cheap leather faster than it happened to honest leather — and because the feeling in his stomach that had arrived with the deletion of the Thread of Truth had not left in six weeks, and movement helped.

Balogun market on a Saturday was not a place you went if you valued silence, personal space, or any reliable orientation to your own location within the city. It was a vast, dense, three-dimensional commerce — stalls above stalls, goods hanging from lines above the walkways, traders operating at volumes calibrated to be heard over all other traders simultaneously. The smell was everything at once: the sharp chemical dye of synthetic fabric, the sweetness of cheap soap, the reek of the open gutter running along the market's western boundary, and underneath all of it the human warmth of ten thousand people in a space designed for three thousand.

Temi moved through it with his hands in his pockets and his collar up against the noise.

He saw her in the fourth aisle.

She was not remarkable in any way that would have made her visible across a crowded market under ordinary circumstances. She was a woman of perhaps thirty-five, in a yellow blouse, with a baby tied to her back in a wrapper. The baby was asleep — the particular boneless sleep of an infant completely surrendered to the warmth of the body carrying it, its head lolling gently with each of the mother's steps. The wrapper holding the baby was Aso-Oke. The pattern was the Idanre geometry — his grandfather's pattern, the one Temi had programmed for the festival order.

He stopped walking.

He was watching the woman move through the market crowd, the baby sleeping on her back, the wrapper doing what a good wrapper does — bearing weight, distributing it, making the carried thing feel safe. She reached a stall selling plastic goods and paused to look at the displayed items, reaching out with one hand to examine something, and in the reaching she turned slightly, and the edge of her wrapper caught on a nail protruding from the stall's wooden post.

It was a small nail. A finishing nail, perhaps, or a tack left from some prior construction, the kind of small hardware that every market stall accumulates in its surfaces over years of improvised additions. It caught the corner of the wrapper and the woman continued moving, and the catch became a pull.

The wrapper came apart.

Not at the nail point — not a tear at the point of contact. Along the internal structure, or rather along the place where the internal structure should have been. The weft threads, unsupported by the Thread of Truth's binding lattice, had nothing to hold them in their positions under lateral stress, and when the lateral stress came they yielded to it, and the yielding travelled through the fabric with the speed of something that had been waiting for the first available excuse. The cloth did not tear so much as it disarticulated — the pattern holding briefly in the surface threads while the structure beneath it gave way, and then the pattern going too, the geometry that had been woven in Ondo state for centuries dissolving in one second of applied force into a ragged, confused sprawl of thread.

The baby dropped three inches before the woman's arm shot back and caught it.

Three inches. The baby woke, startled, and cried — one sharp, high note of protest at the interruption of its sleep. The mother had the child in both arms before Temi had processed what he had seen. She was checking the baby's head, her hands moving over it with the frantic, precise attention of a mother whose fear has been converted by the child's safety into a trembling that she pressed into the baby's hair. The baby stopped crying. The woman held it against her chest and closed her eyes for a moment.

Temi stood in the market aisle with the noise of Balogun around him and the wrapper in pieces on the stall's wooden post and the floor and he looked at his hands.

He turned them over. He looked at the palms — clean, emptied, unwritten by three months in Lagos. He looked at the bonuses cheque in his jacket pocket, which he could feel without touching it, the way you feel a stone in a shoe even when the stone is very small. He touched the torn fabric. It was synthetic in his fingers — the feel of polyester, which is the feel of something that has been made to look like something without being it, and which the body identifies correctly without needing the mind to explain it. Dead. Weightless. A surface without a structure.

He stood there until the woman and the baby had moved away, until the torn fabric had been trampled by other feet, until the market had absorbed the interruption entirely as it absorbed everything — without pause, without record.

Then he turned around and went back to the factory.

He did not take the bus. He walked the long way, through the noise and the exhaust and the afternoon heat, because he needed the walking and because he did not want to arrive before he had decided what to do and he could not decide what to do without the walking. By the time he reached the building in Oshodi his shoes were dusty and his jacket was damp at the back with sweat and the feeling in his stomach was no longer in his stomach but had moved upward — past his ribcage, into his chest, filling the cavity where the cold note had struck on the highway, spreading.

Mr. Coker's office was on the second floor. Temi climbed the stairs. He did not knock — he opened the door. Mr. Coker looked up from his desk with an expression of mild surprise that did not fully disguise the more complex calculation happening behind it.

Temi reached into his jacket pocket and took out the bonus cheque.

He set it on the desk between Mr. Coker's hands — the soft hands, the clean hands, the hands that destroyed things without ever being marked by the destruction. He did not speak. He looked at Mr. Coker and Mr. Coker looked at the cheque and then at Temi, and whatever Mr. Coker was about to say, Temi turned around and walked out before it arrived.

On the stairs he passed Chukwu, who looked at his face and stepped to the side without asking any questions.

On the street he took off the jacket and folded it over his arm. The air of Oshodi fell on him — the exhaust, the generator smoke, the complicated smell of a city that produces everything except what it needs — and he walked east, toward the bus park, with his jacket over his arm and his hands at his sides and the sourness in his chest beginning, barely, to change its quality.

## Chapter 8: The Broken Soles

The bus to Ondo did not have a seat for him.

Not because it was full — it was nearly full, which in Lagos bus mathematics meant there was exactly one remaining space, a middle seat between a man whose shoulders occupied more than their allotted zone and a woman with a basket on her lap that reduced the available knee room to a theoretical concept — but because Temi had arrived at the park with only what the jacket pocket contained, and what the jacket pocket contained was his phone and nothing else, because the bonus cheque was on Mr. Coker's desk and the salary savings were in an envelope under the mattress in a room he was not going to return to.

He stood at the bus park for twenty minutes working this problem.

He was not good at asking for help. He had never had a useful practice in it — Baba Odun gave what was needed before it was requested, and in Idanre the community's assistance moved through the compound walls the way the mist moved, without being summoned. In Lagos no one gave what was not requested, and even what was requested arrived at a cost and a delay that made the asking feel more expensive than the alternative.

He asked.

A yam trader from Ondo State, sitting on top of his truck in the park, listened to Temi's story with the economical attention of a man who has heard many stories and developed an efficient algorithm for identifying the ones that are true. He looked at Temi's shoes — the city shoes, the thin soles, the pointing toes. He looked at Temi's hands. He looked at his face.

"Odun's grandson?" he said.

"Yes sir."

The man made a sound that was not quite a laugh. "I sold my first yam crop at the market beside your grandfather's weaving shed. Thirty years ago." He looked at the sky, then at Temi. "You can sit in the back. It's yams."

"Yes sir."

"You'll smell like a farm by Ore."

"Yes sir."

He rode to Ore in the back of the truck, on a bed of straw between the yam tubers. The truck was not fast and the road was not smooth and the straw was not soft, but the yam smell was clean and earthy and slightly sweet — the smell of something that grew out of honest ground — and after three months of synthetic polyester and expensive cologne, Temi breathed it in with the specific relief of a person returning to a language their mouth had almost forgotten.

At Ore he got a lift in a bus running toward Akure, sitting on a bench in the back between strangers whose elbows and knees found accommodation with his in the wordless negotiation of shared space. The road from Ore to Akure was better than the truck road, but the bus had suspension that had resigned itself to the situation some years ago, and every bump in the road communicated itself directly through the bench and into Temi's spine.

His feet were hurting.

The city shoes, whose stiffness had been their initial crime against his feet, had committed their final crime in the walking from the factory to the Oshodi bus park: the cheap leather of the soles had failed. Not completely — not the falling-off failure of a sole that has given everything — but a structural failure, internal, where the layered materials that should have distributed the force of each step had separated from each other and now shifted fractionally with each footfall, creating a friction that Temi felt in the balls of his feet and along the outer edges where the separation was worst. The right shoe was worse than the left. By the time he reached Akure and began the road to Idanre, both were announcing themselves with each step.

He had no money for new shoes. He had no money for bus fare from Akure to the foot of the hills.

He walked.

The road from Akure to Idanre was eleven kilometres of red laterite, and he walked it in the failing city shoes in the dry season afternoon, and the shoes communicated their failure to his feet with increasing specificity, and he walked, and the mountain grew ahead of him, and the sky above the Idanre Hills had the particular quality of late afternoon — the deepening blue, the gold at the ridgelines, the silence of the upper air that you could feel even from the road below.

His feet were bleeding by the fifth kilometre. Not badly — the shoes had not broken through to the skin, but the friction had raised blisters on both heels and on the ball of the right foot, and the blisters had broken, which is when the leather of a bad shoe truly introduces itself to you. He walked. He thought about the sandals — the worn leather ones he had pushed out of the car window on the highway, the ones that knew his feet, that had been made by three years of these roads into something specific and irreplaceable. He thought about where they were now. Somewhere on the Lagos Ibadan Expressway, in the red dust beside the highway, carrying the impression of his foot in their insoles.

A cloth woven in a hurry is just a rag waiting to tear.

He had not understood, when Baba Odun said it, that the cloth was not only the fabric. That the hurry was not only the speed. That the tearing was not only of the material.

At the ninth kilometre he stopped and took off the shoes. He held them for a moment — the pointed toes, the failed soles, the leather that had been made to look expensive rather than to last — and set them at the side of the road in the red dirt. He walked the last two kilometres barefoot, and the laterite was warm under his feet and occasionally sharp where small stones had worked to the surface, and each step cost him something, and he paid it.

## Chapter 9: The Six Hundred Steps Up

The rain came as he reached the foot of the steps.

Not a heavy rain — the harmattan was not yet finished with Ondo, and in harmattan country a rain was a negotiation rather than a statement. But it was rain: the particular smell of it, the red dust opening to receive each drop with the specificity of something that has been waiting, the first few drops on the stone steps making dark circles that overlapped and merged as the rain thickened. The steps were going to be wet. The rock, which was smooth at the centre from centuries of feet, would be slick.

He stood at the bottom and looked up.

Six hundred steps. He had climbed them hundreds of times. He knew them — he knew the difficult sections, the narrow places, the steps that were set at awkward heights, the places where the rope guide was more than decorative. He had climbed them with yam loads and firewood loads and once with a full drum of water when the tank at the top was empty, which was the hardest thing his legs had ever done. He knew what the summit asked of you and he knew what it gave in return.

He had no shoes.

He lifted his right foot and looked at it. The heel had a blister burst and dried — a raised, reddened patch, the skin tight and tender. The ball of the foot. He set the foot back down and felt the rough stone of the lowest step against it and understood, with clarity, that the six hundred steps barefoot and bleeding was going to be a different proposition than the six hundred steps in worn sandals.

He began to climb.

The rain came harder at the fifteenth step and did not let up. His feet slid on the wet rock twice in the first fifty steps and he caught himself both times on the rope, the jolt of it running up into his shoulder, his body catching up with the fall that almost happened. He slowed down. He found the grip in the edges of the steps where the centre was too smooth, the sides still rough enough to hold. He climbed.

By the one-hundredth step his calf muscles were announcing themselves in the language of muscles that have been asked to work harder than anticipated. Not cramps — not yet — but a deepening awareness, each step adding to a ledger of effort that was building interest. By the two-hundredth step his breathing had changed, the city months having taken something from his mountain lungs that would need to be reclaimed, and he breathed now in the concentrated, deliberate way of someone who is accounting for each breath rather than assuming them.

The rain ran down the rock face in thin streams, finding the channels worn by many rains before it. He was soaked. His jacket was soaked — the folded jacket over his arm had become a wet bundle that he shifted to his other arm, and then, because it was useless and heavy and reminded him of everything it represented, he hung it on the rope guide at the two-hundred-and-fiftieth step and left it there.

At the three-hundredth step, precisely at the halfway point, he saw her.

She was going up. This was the first difference, in the world before. She had a basket on her head, not of yams but of firewood, the pieces rough and irregular, some still carrying dried bark that flaked off with each step. She was moving slowly, which was the only way to move with a load like that on steps like these in rain like this, and her right hand was on the rope guide and her left was free for balance, and the basket was balanced on a cloth ring on her head with the steady, practised assurance of someone for whom this was ordinary.

She stumbled.

Not on a high step — on one of the shallow ones, near the three-hundred-and-twentieth mark, where the rain had made a small river along the tread and the stone beneath was invisible. Her right foot slid and her left compensated and the basket shifted — not falling, not yet, the decades of practice keeping it on her head even through the stumble — but she went down to one knee and the rope guide held her and she stayed there, breathing, her knee on the wet stone, her hand tight on the rope.

Temi was at the three-hundred-and-fifteenth step. He had five steps between them.

He climbed them quickly.

He did not think about what he was doing in the way that requires deliberation. He thought about it the way the body thinks, which is without language, in the movement itself. He was beside her before the decision had been fully articulated and he was crouching, his hand on the basket, steadying it, and then he was standing with the basket on his own head — the weight of it landing in his neck and shoulders with the honest, specific heaviness of real work — and his hand was extended to her.

She looked at him. Her face was wet with rain and streaked with what might have been the water from the bark or might have been something else, and she looked at him with the assessment of a woman who has learned, over many years of being in situations that required help, to quickly identify the quality of the help being offered.

"You," she said. Just that. Not a question.

"Yes, Mama," Temi said. He did not explain. He did not apologize in words — words at the three-hundred-and-fifteenth step in the rain, with three hundred steps still ahead of them, were a luxury neither of them had.

She took his hand. She stood. She looked at the basket on his head, then at him, and something in her expression completed a sentence that neither of them needed to speak aloud.

They climbed.

By the four-hundredth step Temi's calf muscles had moved from announcing themselves to speaking very loudly about their situation, and the basket was a country of its own — its weight had distributed itself down through his neck and spine and legs, so that every part of him was now carrying a share of the load. The basket smelled of rain and bark and the particular cold mineral smell of the Idanre mist, which was thickening around them now as they ascended into cloud. His feet hurt. The heel blister had reopened on the wet rock and he felt it with each step as a specific, clear-edged pain that he acknowledged and continued through.

By the five-hundredth step he could not see the bottom of the steps and he could not see the top.

They were inside the mountain's breath, in the cloud, in the particular enclosed world of the Idanre summit in mist. The rain had eased to a drizzle. His breathing was full and open now — not struggling, but working, the work that belongs to this particular ascent, the honest expenditure of capacity on something real.

At the five-hundred-and-eightieth step the old woman stopped and turned.

"Leave it here," she said, nodding at the basket.

"We're close —"

"My compound is here." She pointed — a wooden gate, barely visible in the mist, set into the rock face at the edge of the step. She had been walking to her own threshold. She had not needed to go to the summit at all.

He lifted the basket from his head and set it against the gate. His neck and shoulders registered the absence of the weight with a vast, specific relief, the way a room registers silence after noise. He stood straight, slowly.

The old woman looked at him for a long time. Then she reached into the fold of her wrapper and produced, without ceremony, a small piece of cloth — blue, old, a strip of Aso-Oke the width of a hand — and she held it out to him.

He took it. He looked at it. The weave was tight and even, the pattern simple, and along the underside — he turned it, and his fingers found it before his eyes did — the Thread of Truth. The structural binding. Present, continuous, invisible from above, making its quiet argument through the quality of what it prevented from happening.

"My husband wove that," she said. "Thirty years ago. Not a thread has moved."

She went inside. The gate closed.

Temi stood at the five-hundred-and-eightieth step with the strip of cloth in his hands and the mist around him and the last twenty steps above him, and he turned the cloth over and over and felt the Thread of Truth beneath his fingers — the thing no one paid for, the thing that held, the thing that made the long invoice.

He climbed the last twenty steps with his feet bleeding and his shoulders aching and the strip of cloth in his closed fist, and at the top the mist received him and the ancient village appeared through it, and the red laterite was the colour of everything that was old and true and still there.

## Chapter 10: The Silent Compound

The compound was quiet in a way that compounds are not usually quiet.

Temi had grown up with the sounds of Baba Odun's compound the way he had grown up with the sounds of the mountain: not as background but as a presence, a specific texture of being that defined the space. The *clack-clack-swoosh* of the loom, which ran from first light to the failing of the evening. The sound of indigo being mixed in the old clay pots — a stirring sound, slow and rhythmic, punctuated by the particular note the wooden ladle made when it struck the pot's interior. The smell of the dye itself, which was less a smell than a climate, bitter and earthy and persistent, finding its way into fabric and skin and the very walls of the compound's mud-brick surround.

None of these sounds were here now.

Temi stood at the gate of the compound and listened to the absence. The mist was still in the village — it was evening and the sun had had started to retreat — and in the mist the silence had a quality almost like presence, like something that had moved in to occupy the space vacated by the working sounds. He pushed the gate open.

The loom was there. It stood in the weaving corner of the compound where it had always stood, against the east wall where the morning light fell first, and where in the old rhythm of things Baba Odun's work was already in progress before Temi was fully awake. The loom was there but it was covered — a cloth, an ordinary blue cotton cloth, laid over the frame and the heddles and the cloth beam, gathering dust at its edges with the patient industry of neglect. Beneath the dust cover, the loom stood the way tools stand when they have not been used for long enough that using them again seems uncertain.

The white cloth was still on the loom. He could see its edge beneath the dust cover — the pure white of the warp threads, the work that had been eleven days in progress when Temi had stood up from the stool and set the shuttle in the dirt. Still there. Waiting.

In the dirt at the foot of the loom, just where he had left it, was the shuttle.

It was weathered. Three months of dry season and then the first rains had worked on the wood, lightening the darker tones and raising the grain slightly, and it had the matte finish of something that has been outside long enough to stop being apologetic about it. But the wood itself was unchanged — the same dense, dark material, the same weight, the same perfect balance. Temi crouched and picked it up.

The weight of it settled in his hand like a fact.

He stood with the shuttle and went to his grandfather's room.

Baba Odun was lying on his mat. He was awake — his eyes were open and tracking the ceiling with the careful, unhurried attention of a man whose external world has contracted to the dimensions of what is visible from horizontal. He was thinner than he had been three months ago. Not dramatically — not the thinness of illness at its most extreme — but the specific thinness of a man whose body has been borrowing from its reserves for some time and is beginning to carry the account on the surface. His hands lay on the blanket beside him.

Temi looked at his grandfather's hands.

They were the same hands. Gnarled at the knuckles, curved from decades of the shuttle's grip, the skin of the palms thick and textured as bark, the indigo deep in the whorls of the fingertips and the creases of the palm — blue and navy and violet, the evening sky that never washed out because it had never been on the surface to begin with. They were the most honest hands Temi had ever seen. They lay on the blanket and they were still, and their stillness was wrong in the way that a loom's stillness is wrong — not rest, but cessation.

"You're back," Baba Odun said. His voice was the same. Whatever the months had taken from his body they had not taken from his voice.

"Yes, Baba."

"The cloth is still on the loom."

"I know."

"I didn't touch it. It is your thread."

Temi sat down beside the mat. He was aware of his own hands — the left holding the shuttle, the right resting on his knee. He looked at them and remembered looking at them in the compound that first morning, smooth and unwritten, and then looking at them on Mr. Coker's desk, cleaner than they had been at home. And now: not clean, not stained, but somewhere between the two — the beginning of callouses on the right palm from the firewood basket, the heel of the hand slightly reddened from the rope guide, a trace of red laterite in the crease between his index finger and thumb.

The beginning of a text.

"I did something," Temi said. He did not look at his grandfather's face. He looked at the shuttle in his hand. "In Lagos. A big order. I programmed your pattern. And then I—" He stopped. He felt the sourness shift in his chest — it had been sitting there since the Balogun market, since the wrapper came apart in the nail, and it had travelled with him through Oshodi and Ore and Akure and eleven kilometres of red laterite road. "I removed the Thread of Truth from the programme. For a bonus. The cloth looked right from the outside and it held nothing on the inside, and it fell apart in the market when a woman was carrying her baby."

The silence in the compound was very large.

"Was the baby hurt?" Baba Odun said.

"No. She caught it."

Another silence.

"Then you still have time," Baba Odun said.

Temi looked up. His grandfather was looking at the ceiling still, but something in the line of his face had changed — not softened exactly, but shifted, the way the mist shifts when the sun works on it from above, not removing it but clarifying what is beneath.

"The weavers here are struggling," Baba Odun said. "The cheap cloth from the city — cloth with no Thread of Truth in it — has flooded the market. The women who used to commission from us are buying from the roadside dealers. And the men who used to commission from us say the price is too high for what they cannot see." His hands moved slightly on the blanket — not weaving, just moving, as if the fingers still held the memory of the motion. "I told you the truth has a long invoice. I didn't tell you that people stop paying while the invoice is still being written."

He looked at Temi. His eyes were the same as they had always been — dark and clear, the eyes of a man who has looked at cloth long enough to see beneath every surface.

"What will you do?" he said.

Temi looked at the shuttle. He stood up.

## Chapter 11: The Thread of Truth

He did not offer grand apologies. He did not make speeches.

He walked across the compound to the loom, and he removed the dust cover, and he sat on the weaving stool, and he looked at the white cloth.

It was exactly as he had left it. Eleven days of work, suspended in the moment of his leaving, the warp threads holding their four hundred-count tension with the patient fidelity of something that had never been told to stop. The last weft row was the last weft row — the pattern at precisely the point where he had set the shuttle down and stood up and said there is no future in these old stones. The Thread of Truth's invisible structure ran the length of the underside up to that row, and then stopped, waiting for the next pass of the binding thread before the next row of pattern could be honestly built.

He looked at the shuttle in his hand.

The weathered wood. The weight, which was the same weight it had always been. He turned it over and felt the surface that forty years of his grandfather's hands had made, the slight polish in the grip area, the matte finish of the rest. He thought about how a tool is changed by honest use — how the shuttle that had come from the maker as simply a piece of shaped wood had become, over decades, an instrument, shaped not by its maker but by the accumulated intention of the work it had been used to do.

He set the shuttle's tip against the waiting warp.

The resistance was familiar. The first pass through the shed — the gap between the upper and lower warp threads, opened by the heddle — required a specific hand, a specific wrist motion, the kind of motion that cannot be performed correctly by a hand that is thinking about it. It has to be known. His hand knew it. Three months in Lagos had not unknowed it, because the body holds what it has genuinely learned in a place that is not available to the will's revision.

*Clack-clack-swoosh*.

The sound arrived in the compound the way a language arrives when you speak your first sentence after a long silence in it — slightly uncertain of its own right to be there, and then, as the compound received it and held it and the mist caught it at the eaves, completely itself.

He settled into the rhythm. He was slower than he had been — the muscles of the weaving required a specific use that had softened in three months, the way any capacity softens when it is not called on, and he could feel the gap between what the motion should be and what it currently was. It did not discourage him. A capacity that has been genuinely built will return, the way a language returns — gradually, and then, one morning, completely.

He wove the pattern row. Then he wove the Thread of Truth.

This was the hard part. Not mechanically — mechanically, the Thread of Truth was a specific sequence of binding passes, a learned procedure. Hard in the other sense: hard the way things are hard when they require you to do them without an audience and without a visible result, when the only person who will ever know they have been done correctly is the one doing them and the cloth itself.

He had thought, on the highway from Lagos, about Mr. Coker's question: who weaves the part that no one sees? He had understood it then as a philosophical question, abstract and rhetorical. He understood it now as a practical one. Who weaves the part that no one sees? The person who understands that the seeing is not the point. The person whose integrity does not require a witness because it is not performing for one. The person whose hands know the difference between a cloth that will hold and a cloth that will not, and who chooses, in the private and unobserved moment, to make the one that will hold.

He wove the Thread of Truth across the underside of the new row.

Then the next row. Then the binding. Row, binding, row, binding — the rhythm of honest work, slow and unglamorous, producing in each iteration something invisible to the casual eye and essential to everything. His shoulders found the right posture. His wrists found the right angle. The shuttle moved through the shed with the specific confidence that comes from correctly positioned effort — not force, not haste, but the gathered intention of a hand that has decided what it is for.

He did not know how long he had been weaving when he became aware that Baba Odun was at the doorway of his room.

The old man had not risen from his mat unaided in — Temi did not know how long. Weeks, from the look of the compound. But he was standing in the doorway, his hand on the frame, his face turned toward the loom with the expression of a man who is listening to something he had begun to believe he would not hear again. His indigo-stained hands were visible at the doorframe, the gnarled fingers curled lightly around the wood.

He looked at Temi. He looked at the cloth.

He did not speak.

Temi did not speak.

The loom spoke: *clack-clack-swoosh, clack-clack-swoosh*, the sound of the mountain breathing, the sound of a thread placed correctly in the warp of things, the sound that the compound had been holding the shape of during its silence as if saving the space for it to return.

After a long time, Baba Odun moved from the doorway to the stool beside the dye pots. He sat. He did not pick up a stirring ladle. He simply sat and watched his grandson weave, in the way that masters watch when watching is itself a form of instruction — when what they are communicating is not a correction but a recognition.

Temi looked up once, briefly, from the loom.

His grandfather's face in the evening light had the quality of the old things here — the rock face, the worn steps, the wooden shuttle — that quality of having been through enough seasons that the surface no longer distinguished between weathering and character, because they had become the same thing. He was smiling. Not largely — not the expansive smile of relief or the demonstrative smile of approval. A small smile, the kind that lives at the corners of the eyes rather than the mouth, the kind that is less an expression and more a report.

Temi looked back at the cloth.

His hands were changing. He could feel it — not see it yet, not the indigo stains that would come with the dye work when the cloth was finished and the dyeing began, but the beginning of the callouses, the specific roughening of the palm at the shuttle-grip, the slight ridging of the index finger where the thread ran over it. His hands were starting to say something. The text was beginning.

Outside, the mist was descending on the summit. Below, the town was getting absorbed into the cloud. The mountain was doing what it did every evening: concealing the distance between up here and down there, the vertical fact of the six hundred steps, the geography of the choice.

Temi looked at the distance and felt, for the first time, no pull toward it.

Not because the city had been defeated or the ambition had been killed or the world below had ceased to be real. But because you cannot be pulled toward something you have already travelled to and returned from with a clear understanding of what it contained. He had gone down the mountain looking for gold and he had found brass, and he had found what brass does when it meets the first real pressure: it shines until it doesn't, and beneath the shine there is nothing with a long invoice.

The gold was here. It was in the Thread of Truth, invisible and essential. It was in his grandfather's hands, which were the most honest things he had ever seen. It was in the morning's slow work, which produced nothing that any passing buyer could immediately appreciate and which would outlast every garment in Mr. Coker's premium catalogue by a generation.

*Clack-clack-swoosh*.

He wove. The thread found its place in the warp. The pattern advanced by one row, and beneath it, invisible, the binding held.

The stones were old. But the truth in them — the truth that had been woven into cloth on this mountain for as long as anyone could trace the weaving — that would hold them together. Not because anyone was watching. Not because the invoice was being paid. Because the Thread of Truth, once placed correctly, does not move. It holds because it is there, and it is there because someone chose, in the private moment, to put it there.

Baba Odun closed his eyes in the evening light.

Temi's hands found their rhythm.

The mountain breathed.
