## Part One: The Green Roof of the World

***

### Chapter One: The Breath of the Mountain

Before the sun cleared the ridge, the grass on the Mambilla Plateau was already trying to bite.

Every blade was glazed with ice-dew, a thin skin of frozen water that caught the weak first light and shattered it into a thousand cold sparks. The earth smelled of the night — wet black soil, cow dung, the faint ghost of woodsmoke drifting from the chimney of the farmhouse — and the air that came off the peaks above was the kind of cold that did not ask permission. It simply entered you. It went straight through the weave of your sweater, past the cotton of your vest, and settled into your bones as if it lived there and was returning home.

Bello felt it in his fingers first.

He was ten years old, and his hands were pressed against the warm side of a cow named Patience, pulling at her in the long, rhythmic way his father had shown him. The warmth travelled up through his palms and into his wrists, and for a few seconds each minute his fingers forgot that it was cold. Then he would switch sides, and the cold would remember him.

"You're going too fast," said a voice beside him.

Bello did not look up. He did not need to. He knew the voice the way he knew the sound of his own heartbeat — so familiar it was almost invisible.

Zara, his nine-year-old sister, was crouched against the next cow with her small hands working steadily, her chin almost touching the warm brown flank. She wore a yellow woollen hat pulled down so far that only her eyes and the very tip of her nose were visible. Her eyes were the sharpest thing on the plateau.

"I am not going too fast," Bello said. "I am going at the right speed."

"You spilled some."

Bello glanced at the pail. A thin line of white ran down the outside. He had not noticed. Something hot and sudden bloomed in his ears — a deep, embarrassing heat that had nothing to do with the cold — and he straightened his back and lifted his chin, as if the extra height might hide the spill.

"A little," he said. "That is different from a lot."

"Baba says—"

"I know what Baba says."

His father was moving further down the line of cows, his big hands steady and certain, his old rubber boots sucking at the mud with each slow step. The sound was like a word repeated over and over: shuck, shuck, shuck. Emmanuel Bulus — Baba to his children, Malam Bulus to the other farmers — was a tall man made of patient angles. He did not rush. He did not raise his voice. He had a way of being in a place that made the animals calm, as if his presence said: nothing terrible will happen here today.

His staff was leaning against the fence post at the end of the row.

It was made from mountain mahogany — dark red-brown wood, dense and heavy, with a crooked top that Baba had smoothed by years of holding. Bello and Zara both knew its smell: woodsmoke and cattle oil, old work, honest sweat. On cold evenings, Baba rested his chin on its top and watched the herd the way a captain watches the sea. It was the truest thing he owned.

Bello watched his father's back and felt the familiar, complicated feeling rise in his chest — part love, part wanting. He worked his hands faster against the cow.

"Still spilling," Zara said, without turning her head.

"Still watching instead of working," Bello replied.

She turned her head then, just enough to show one eye around the edge of the yellow hat. The eye was not angry. It was something more precise than angry.

"I have filled half my pail," she said. "You have filled a third of yours and the ground has received the rest."

Bello opened his mouth and closed it. He could not think of the correct answer. He turned back to his cow and worked in silence, and the cold came back into his fingers as if it, too, were making a point.

### Chapter Two: A Drop of Milk in the Grass

The new calf arrived at the very end of the night.

Bello heard it first — a thin, high sound from the far end of the pen, too light to be one of the cows, too insistent to be the wind. He sat up from his sleeping mat and listened, his heart already awake before the rest of him had caught up.

By the time he reached the pen in his bare feet, his father was already there, crouching in the mud with his lantern held low. Zara appeared at Bello's shoulder a moment later, breathing hard from running, wearing her yellow hat over her sleeping clothes.

And there, between the legs of the mother cow, blinking at the lantern with large and bewildered eyes, was the most impossible calf any of them had ever seen.

It was entirely white.

Not the pale brown-white of the older calves, or the patchy white of the spotted ones. This calf was the white of the fog that came off the peaks in the afternoon — dense, absolute, a white that seemed to hold light inside it rather than simply reflect it. It wobbled on its four new legs, each leg making its own independent decision about which direction to point, and it blinked at the world as though it had arrived by mistake and was still deciding whether to stay.

"Eh," Baba breathed. Just that one quiet syllable, but it held everything.

"It's white," Zara said.

"It's not white," Bello said. "It is — it is like the cloud. Like the cloud that comes down from the peaks."

Baba looked at him. Then he looked back at the calf. He smiled in the slow, unhurried way he had — not a sudden thing but a gradual one, like the morning arriving.

"Cloud," Baba said. "Yes. That is what he is."

The calf turned at the sound of the name, its ears swivelling, and then it took three uncertain steps toward Bello and pressed its pink nose against his hand. The tongue that came out a moment later was rough as tree bark — a sandpaper warmth that moved across Bello's palm and left a trail of salt-damp that the cold air dried instantly.

Bello had not expected that. He had not expected the realness of it — the specific, physical fact of an animal that had not existed yesterday choosing his hand right now, this exact cold morning. Something moved in his chest that was not pride and was not competition. It was simpler than both.

He looked at Zara. She was already on her knees beside the calf, her yellow hat nearly touching its ear, her face doing something it did not often do. It was open. Unguarded. She looked, for those few seconds, exactly her age.

The calf turned and licked her cheek.

Zara made a sound that was neither a laugh nor a gasp but something between the two, a small surprised noise that she immediately tried to cover with her hand — but the sound had already escaped, and it hung in the cold lantern-lit air between them.

Bello said nothing. He looked at his hand where the tongue had been. The salt was still there.

They knelt together in the cold mud and watched Cloud find his legs, and for a few minutes there was no rivalry between them. Only the calf, and the lantern, and the warm breath of the herd rising in small white clouds against the black Mambilla sky.

### Chapter Three: The Weight of the Wood

The day Baba went to Gembu market, he woke the children before the mist had lifted from the valley.

They stood in the doorway of the farmhouse while he loaded the old pickup truck — seed bags, empty jerry cans, the repair tools for the fence posts. The plateau stretched out behind him, green and vast and wrapped in the white morning haze, the tea farms on the slopes below appearing and disappearing as the mist shifted. It smelled of wet earth and the sharp, minty bruise of crushed tea leaves carried up from the valley by the slow wind.

"I will be back before the fog comes down," Baba said. He was tying the last rope over the seed bags, not looking at either of them, but his voice was directed at both. "Watch the herd. Keep Cloud close to the mother. Do not leave the gate open."

"Yes, Baba," they said together.

He put on his coat. He picked up his keys. And then he stopped.

He walked to the fence where his staff was leaning and looked at it for a moment. His hand touched it once, the way you touch something you have carried for years — not to check if it is still there but simply to acknowledge it. Then he left it against the fence and turned back to his children.

"Be good to each other," he said. It was not a command, exactly. It was more like a door being left open.

He climbed into the truck. The engine coughed and caught. They watched the truck move down the winding red road toward the valley, getting smaller and smaller until the mist closed over it like water closing over a stone.

The silence it left behind was large.

Then, slowly, both children turned and looked at the staff.

It stood against the fence post, slightly taller than either of them, its dark wood vivid against the grey morning. It was not pointing at anyone. It was just standing there, the way an important object stands when it knows it is important.

The air between the children tightened.

Bello cleared his throat. "I should hold it," he said. "I am the elder one. Baba always gives me the harder work."

"Baba always gives me the careful work," Zara said. Her voice was level. "Watching and counting. Sharp eyes. He has said so himself."

"The staff goes with the one who leads."

"The staff goes with the one who will not drop it."

Neither moved. The morning fog drifted between them, soft and indifferent.

Somewhere in the pen, Cloud made a small sound — a light, wondering call, as if asking a question of the morning. Neither child answered it. They were looking at each other, and the staff was looking at both of them, and the plateau was very large and very quiet.

## Part Two: The Fog and the Folly

***

### Chapter Four: The Tug-of-War

Bello moved first.

He covered the distance to the fence in four steps and closed his hands around the staff. The wood was cool and solid. It felt, in his palms, like authority. His neck straightened. His chin came up. His ears went faintly warm — not the embarrassed heat from the morning, but the heat of a different kind of pride. A certain, settled heat.

"I am the elder," he said. "I am in charge. That is how it works."

Zara was already beside him. Her hand wrapped around the staff below his. She pulled, not hard, but with a steadiness that was harder to resist than force.

"Being older is not the same as being wiser," she said. "You spilled this morning."

"That was milk. This is different."

"Everything is connected. Baba says that too."

They stood there, both hands on the wood, neither pulling hard enough to win and neither letting go. The morning had grown around them, the sun higher now but still muted behind the high clouds, the fog beginning to lift off the valley below only to reform above them at the peaks. Somewhere behind them the herd was moving, the low contented sound of cows at grass, the occasional soft bell of the one cow who wore it.

They were not listening to the herd.

"Let go, Zara."

"You let go, Bello."

The arguing was physical — not just words but weight, two bodies leaning in opposite directions on the same piece of wood, boots scrabbling for grip in the wet morning mud. Bello's right boot found a slick patch where the cow dung and the rainwater had made a kind of paste, and his heel went sideways. He overcorrected. Zara, braced the other way, was pulled off balance by his sudden shift. They staggered. The staff swung between them like a compass needle trying to find north.

And the staff's lower end knocked the wooden gate.

Not hard. Not with any intent. Just a tap — the kind of accidental contact that happens when two people are not paying attention to anything except each other. But the gate's iron latch, old and slightly bent from the last time it had caught in a freeze, was resting at precisely the angle where such a tap was enough. The latch bumped up. The gate swung a few inches open on its hinges. The hinges made no sound at all.

Bello regained his footing. Zara regained hers. They were still breathing hard, still holding the staff between them, still looking at each other with the particular determination of children in an argument that neither wants to be the first to feel foolish about.

Outside the open gate, a patch of bright green clover grew in the long grass of the field. The clover was new — the rain of the last two days had brought it up overnight — and it was the specific shade of green that means tender and sweet and more nutritious than anything in the pen.

A white shape moved quietly through the gap.

### Chapter Five: The Cloud-Swallower

Zara noticed the stillness first.

The particular kind of stillness — the absence of a small, moving white presence in the corner of her eye. She had been aware of Cloud without consciously watching him, the way you are aware of the brightest thing in a room even when you are not looking directly at it. And now that brightness had stopped moving, stopped making its small sounds, stopped existing in the part of her vision where it had always been.

She turned. The pen. The cows. Brown and black and dappled. No white.

"Bello."

Her voice had changed. Bello heard the change before he processed the word. He let go of the staff.

"What?"

"Count the calves."

He turned. He counted. His lips moved. He counted again.

The stone arrived behind his belly button at the same moment it arrived behind hers — a cold, dense, particular weight that had nothing to do with anything he had eaten. It sat there, very still, and radiated cold outward in all directions.

"He was here," Bello said. "He was here just—"

"The gate."

They both looked at the gate standing open, innocent and unhelpful, its bent latch catching the pale morning light.

Neither of them said what both of them knew: that this was their fault, and that the fault was shared equally, and that there was no version of events in which either of them came out clean.

Then the weather decided to make things worse.

On the Mambilla Plateau, the afternoon fog does not arrive the way fog arrives in other places — slowly, apologetically, creeping in from the edges. On Mambilla, it descends. One moment the sky is a flat grey ceiling. The next, the ceiling has come down to meet the earth, and everything above your knees has vanished. The locals call it the Cloud-Swallower. It earns the name.

It came from the peaks above them, pouring down the ridge like white water, filling the hollows first and then the slopes and then the open plateau fields in a matter of minutes. The tea farms below disappeared. The winding red road disappeared. The far fence disappeared. The clover field — the place where Cloud had most likely gone — disappeared.

Bello picked up the staff. He was not thinking about the argument. He was not thinking about being the elder. He was thinking about the small white calf out in that white nothing.

"I will go," he said. "I run faster."

"Bello, wait—"

"Stay here. Guard the others. I will find him." He was already moving. "I run faster," he said again, as if saying it twice made it more true.

He sprinted into the white void and vanished in four steps.

Zara stood at the open gate with the fog filling in around her like water. The cold stone behind her belly button had not moved. She stared at the place where her brother had been.

Then she closed the gate carefully. The latch caught. She checked it twice.

She looked at the fog. She looked at the herd. She looked at the long damp grass and the mud of the plateau, which held, like any wet earth holds, the clear and patient record of everything that had recently walked across it.

She knelt down.

### Chapter Six: The Invisible World

Ten steps into the fog, Bello's speed became a problem.

He could not see the ground beneath his feet clearly enough to trust it, but he was running at the pace he used on the open road, and the two things were incompatible. He slowed, reluctantly, from a sprint to a jog, and then from a jog to a fast walk, and then from a fast walk to a stop, because he had arrived at the place where he could not tell which direction he had come from.

The fog on Mambilla was not transparent. It was not the thin, romantic mist of lower elevations that lets you see the shapes of trees and walls and makes everything look like a painting. This fog was a substance. It pressed against your eyes and gave back nothing but white. Every ten feet was its own small world, complete and sealed, with no information about what lay beyond it.

Bello turned in a slow circle. All four directions were identical. White, and the dark shapes of grass blades emerging from it close to his feet, and then white again.

The confidence that had sent him sprinting out of the gate — I run faster, I run faster — had thinned to almost nothing. In its place was a new sensation, arriving in his legs without his permission: a fine, constant trembling that he could not stop even when he stood perfectly still. His knees were conducting a private conversation with themselves about how frightened they were. The rest of him pretended not to listen.

He heard something.

A rustle to his left — something moving in the grass — and the hot certainty came back in a sudden rush. The calf. He moved toward the sound, quickly, the trembling in his knees overridden by the surge of being sure. He moved faster. He was almost running again.

He stepped on something that was not solid earth.

The wet moss on the rocky edge was a particular green that looked, in the fog, exactly like the wet grass. His boot landed on it at the worst possible angle. The moss had the same relationship with friction that oil does with water, which is to say: none.

The plateau disappeared from beneath his feet.

He had just enough time to register the sound of the staff leaving his hand before the slope took him.

## Part Three: The Fall and the Finding

***

### Chapter Seven: The Slippery Slope

He did not fall neatly.

There was nothing neat about it — no graceful roll, no controlled tumble. There was mud, and rock, and the sharp specific bite of a stone edge against his shoulder, and the way the sky and the earth kept trading places with each other, and the terrible speed of it, the way the slope had its own agenda and cared nothing for his.

He stopped when a narrow ledge of rock stopped him.

He lay in the mud for a moment with his face turned sideways, looking at a single blade of wet grass at eye level. His right ankle was sending information to his brain, and the information was clear and unwelcome: something had gone wrong with it. Not broken — he did not think broken — but twisted in a way that announced itself loudly with every small movement, a sharp and insistent pain that started at the ankle and climbed all the way up to his hip.

He tried to stand. His ankle disagreed. He sat back down in the mud.

The fog was complete around him. He could see perhaps eight feet in any direction. Above him, the slope he had fallen down was invisible — just white, and the sound of the wind passing across the plateau somewhere above.

The staff was gone. He had heard it leave his hand. He could not see it anywhere.

Bello sat in the cold mud on the Mambilla plateau with his ankle swelling and the fog closing in and not a single direction to go that he could trust, and the tears that came were not the tears of a boy who has been hurt. They were the tears of a boy who has understood something too late and cannot unknow it.

He had run into the fog without a plan. He had run because running felt like doing something, and doing something felt better than admitting that he did not know what to do. He had been so certain that speed was the same as capability, that being first was the same as being right.

His ankle hurt. The staff was somewhere in the fog. And out there, somewhere — cold, white, and frightened — was a small calf that needed finding by someone who was not currently sitting in the mud.

The tears ran warm down his cold face and fell into the mud beside him.

He did not bother to wipe them away. There was no one to see them.

### Chapter Eight: The Slow Tracker

Zara did not run.

She stood at the gate for thirty seconds, which is not a long time but is long enough for someone who uses their eyes well to see things that people in a hurry miss. Then she got down on her hands and knees at the edge of the gate and looked at the mud.

Two sets of tracks. The calf's, small and cloven, leading toward the clover field to the east. And Bello's, deep and wide and far apart — the track of someone running without looking at his feet — leading northwest, which was not where the clover was, and was not where the calf had gone.

She felt something move in her chest that was complicated and not entirely kind. She let it move. Then she made a decision, and the decision was this: she would find Bello first, because a lost calf was a problem but a lost brother was a different kind of problem entirely, and you solve the closer problem before you solve the further one.

She followed his tracks.

She did not run. She moved at a pace that allowed her eyes to stay ahead of her feet, checking each footfall before she committed to it, reading the mud the way Baba had taught her to read it — the depth of the print tells you the speed, the direction of the splash tells you the lean of the body, the gap between prints tells you the length of the stride. Bello had been moving very fast. She could see where his pace had changed, becoming more urgent, and then where it had changed again into something more careless.

The fog was cold against her face. Inside it, all sound was muffled and strange. The distant bell of the cow back in the pen came to her like a sound heard underwater — real, but reduced. A bird called somewhere above her and the sound fell flat the moment it left the bird's throat.

She thought about Bello's face when Baba left. The way he had looked at the staff. She thought about how the rivalry between them was not a simple thing — it was made of love, the way a seed is made of the tree it will become. She knew that. She suspected he knew it too. But knowing something and living by it were separated by a distance that was sometimes very large.

Her knee found a rock and she hissed through her teeth and stopped. She looked at the ground carefully. Then she looked up.

The tracks had stopped. There was a smear of mud on a large flat rock — the kind of smear that a sliding boot leaves — and below the rock, the slope dropped away steeply.

Zara's stomach went cold.

She tested the ground with her foot, found solid purchase, and looked over the edge of the drop very carefully.

She did not see him immediately. The fog was dense in the hollow. But she heard — just under the sound of the wind — something she recognised the way you recognise the sound of your own name.

### Chapter Nine: The Broken Stick

"Bello."

His name, just once. Not loud. She said it the way you say the name of something you were worried you wouldn't find.

He heard her. He stopped crying and went very still, the way you go still when you are embarrassed and hoping to be smaller. He looked up and could not see her through the fog, but the voice had come from above him and to the right.

"Here," he said. His voice was rougher than he intended. "I'm here."

She came down the slope slowly, testing each foothold. She was wearing her yellow hat and her coat, and when she emerged from the white fog above him her face told him nothing about what she intended to do — not triumph, not pity, not the particular expression he had been bracing for. The expression that says: I told you so.

It was not that expression.

She crouched beside him in the mud and looked at his ankle. It had already swollen enough to press against the inside of his boot. She pressed two careful fingers against the swelling, not hard. He made a sound through his nose.

"Not broken," she said. "Twisted." She sat back on her heels. "Can you stand?"

"I was going to."

She looked at him. She had the kind of patience that sometimes made him feel seen in ways he wasn't sure he wanted.

"Try," she said.

He tried. He got halfway up and the ankle surrendered, and he was going back down into the mud when her arm came under his and stopped him. She was smaller than him — had always been smaller, a fact he had used in arguments since they were very young — but the arm that held him was steady in the mud and she did not buckle.

He leaned on her. He did not thank her. She did not say anything about it.

Then she moved away from him slightly and looked at the slope above, scanning slowly. He watched her eyes move — the systematic, unhurried movement of someone who has taught herself to look the way other people have taught themselves to listen. The yellow hat turned left, then right, then stopped.

She pushed through a low tangle of wet bushes and came back holding the mahogany staff. It was muddy from the halfway up. She turned it over in her hands, then held it out to him.

Not like an accusation. Not like a reminder of who had been holding it when this began. Just held it out, wood-first, the way you offer something to someone who needs it.

Bello looked at the staff in her hand. He looked at his sister's face. The hot feeling in his ears was back — but it was a different heat than before. Smaller, and lower, and harder to name. It lived somewhere near the chest rather than the surface of the skin.

He took the staff. He leaned his weight on it and it held him.

"The calf," he said.

"I know," she said. "Come."

## Part Four: The Shared Burden

***

### Chapter Ten: The Song in the Dark

They moved through the fog slowly, his weight distributed between the staff on his right and her shoulder on his left.

He was heavier than she expected. She was stronger than he remembered. Neither observation was said aloud.

The fog had not thinned. If anything the plateau was deeper in it now, the late afternoon pressing the cloud lower, the temperature dropping as the sun moved behind the high ridgeline. Their breath came out in small white puffs that mixed with the mist and disappeared. The mountain mahogany staff left small round holes in the mud with each placement — a new track, more deliberate than the one Bello had made alone, less reckless, going somewhere.

"Which way did the tracks go?" Bello asked.

"East. Toward the clover field."

"That's back toward the pen. We went northwest."

"I know. That's why I went northwest first."

He didn't answer that. There was no useful answer to it.

They moved east, correcting their course by the one compass Zara had that Bello did not: the direction of the wind, which she knew from years of standing on this plateau came down from the northeast peaks in the afternoon and shifted south as the evening came. The wind on her left cheek meant east. She kept it on her left cheek.

It was cold enough now that Bello's teeth had started performing on their own — a fine, involuntary percussion. He felt it in his jaw and his throat and down into his chest. His ankle was a steady declaration of discomfort, not screaming but not quiet.

He was thinking.

Baba had a call he used with the newborns. Not words — just a sound, a high, clicking sequence that he repeated until the calf's ears found it and the calf moved toward it. Bello had heard it so many times that it lived inside him without effort, the way a song you've known your whole life lives in you even when you haven't thought of it for months.

He started to make the sound.

It came out thin at first, affected by the cold and the chattering teeth, but he steadied it. A high clicking rhythm, two-three-two, repeated. The fog swallowed the sound immediately — but so did the fog swallow everything, and the question was not whether the sound carried far but whether there was something close enough to hear it.

Zara stopped.

She put her hand on his arm — not to silence him but to still their movement, to reduce the sound of their own boots in the mud so that there was more room in the air for other things.

They listened.

Then: a sound. Not a call — not the full, high sound of a calf in distress. Something smaller. A sustained, thin note, barely above the wind. The sound of a frightened animal that has been making noise for a long time and whose voice is beginning to fail.

Zara's hand tightened on Bello's arm. Her eyes found the direction before the rest of her did.

"There," she said. "Twenty feet. The bramble at the edge of the drop."

He could not see it. She was already moving.

### Chapter Eleven: The Three-Legged Journey

Cloud was very cold.

He had walked through the open gate in pursuit of the green clover and found it, and eaten some, and then the fog had come down and the clover field had stopped being a field and become a small white room with no door. He had walked in one direction, which had not been helpful, and then in another direction, which had been worse, until the bramble at the edge of the drop had caught his left foreleg and held him.

He had been there for a long time, getting colder.

When Zara reached him, he was shivering in a total, committed way, every part of him oscillating, his white coat darker at the legs where the mud had climbed. His eyes found her immediately — those large, careful eyes, still holding their particular expression of baffled inquiry — and the thin sound he had been making became something higher and more urgent.

She untangled his leg from the bramble with careful hands. She spoke to him low and quiet, not words but sounds — the specific register of a human voice being gentle on purpose. He allowed this. He pressed his wet nose against her sleeve.

"He can't walk properly," she said. "The leg is not broken — it just needs to know which way to go."

"We have to carry him."

She looked at Cloud. Cloud was a calf, and calves look light because they are young and they have large eyes, but they are not light. They weigh what they weigh, which is considerably more than they appear to.

She bent her knees and got her arms under him and stood up.

Her legs understood the weight immediately. They sent an honest report to the rest of her body: this is very heavy, and we would like to discuss it. Her back made a secondary complaint. Her shoulders registered their position and added a note.

She kept standing.

Cloud, for his part, submitted. He was cold and he was tired and he had found two humans he recognised, and the business of being carried, though undignified, was at least warm.

Bello, beside her, saw her knees bend under the weight. He watched her back straighten with effort. He looked at his staff, and at his twisted ankle, and at his sister.

He could not carry the calf. He had tried taking a step without leaning on the staff and the ankle had given its decisive answer.

But he could give her what he had.

He moved to her right side — her weaker side, the one that was already beginning to lean under the calf's weight — and put his free arm around her waist. Not to take the calf from her. Just to be a wall she could lean against. Just to divide the work of staying upright.

"Go," he said.

They went.

The staff went into the mud, and Bello's weight went onto the staff, and Zara's weight went partially onto Bello, and the calf's weight went onto Zara, and the plateau received all of it without comment, because the plateau had been receiving the weight of living things for longer than any of them could imagine, and it knew how this worked.

They did not speak. Speech used breath that was needed for moving.

The wind kept Zara's left cheek, and her left cheek kept them east.

The fog was a white country they were passing through together, and the gate was somewhere ahead in it, and the pen was beyond the gate, and inside the pen there was a mother cow whose white calf she had last seen walking away through a gap, and the fence had a lantern post, and behind the lantern post there was a farmhouse, and inside the farmhouse there was a fire, and the fire was warm.

They walked toward the warmth. One body at a time, three sets of legs between them.

Cloud stopped shivering somewhere around the halfway point. The warmth of the two children holding him was enough to begin the process.

### Chapter Twelve: The Warm Hearth

The fog did not lift cleanly. It retreated in pieces, showing them the fence post first, then the shape of the lantern above the gate, and then the gate itself — and the figure standing beside it.

Baba was very still.

He was wearing his coat and his boots, and his keys were in his hand, and the way he was standing — slightly forward, weight on his toes, the posture of a man ready to move in any direction — told both children that he had been standing there for some time, having run through the calculations that a parent runs through when the children are not where they should be and the fog has come down.

His eyes found Cloud first. Then Bello's ankle, and the staff, and the angle of his weight. Then Zara's arms and back and the careful, exhausted set of her knees. He read all of it in the way he read everything — without rushing, without noise, thoroughly.

He came to them and took the calf from Zara's arms. She did not resist. Her arms fell to her sides and she stood for a moment simply breathing, her back slowly receiving the news that the weight was gone.

Baba looked at the calf, at the leg, at the mud. Then he looked at both children.

"Who left the gate unlatched?" he said.

It was a quiet question. There was no accusation built into it — just the question, open and waiting.

Two seconds of silence. In two seconds, each child performed a rapid calculation: the argument about the staff, the wrestling for possession, the moment the staff knocked the gate, the latch lifting.

"I did, Baba," Bello said.

He said it before he had fully decided to say it — or perhaps deciding and saying had, for once, happened at the same time. It was not a comfortable thing to say. But the cold stone behind his belly button, which had been sitting there since the moment they'd noticed Cloud was gone, shifted slightly when the words were out. Not gone. Just moved.

"No." Zara's voice. Immediate. "We both did. We were fighting. The staff hit the gate because we were both holding it and not watching anything else."

Baba looked at them both for a long time.

He did not say well done. He did not say I am proud. He was not that kind of father — not because he did not feel those things, but because he understood that some moments are not for adding to, and this was one of them. The truth had been spoken by both children at the same time, each one refusing to let the other carry it alone, and that was its own complete thing.

He nodded, once, and passed the calf to the mother cow who had moved to the fence and was pressing her nose through the gap, making a sound low in her throat. Cloud, reunited with the warmth of the herd, pressed his own face against his mother's neck and was still.

Then Baba put his arm around Bello and walked him inside.

The farmhouse was warm in the particular way that farmhouses on the plateau are warm — not the even warmth of a controlled thing, but the living warmth of an actual fire in an actual hearth, a fire fed with actual wood, crackling and shifting and filling the room with the smell of it, the orange and amber light moving on the walls.

Bello's boot came off with some difficulty. His ankle, freed from the leather, was impressively unhappy about the whole situation, swollen and violet at the outer edge. Baba's hands on it were firm and knowing — he had twisted ankles on this plateau himself, in this weather, on this rock, and he knew what he was looking at. He wrapped it in a length of cloth from the shelf, steadily and without comment.

Zara came in with the hot water for the tea.

She made it strong, the way Baba liked, with the dried tea leaves they grew on the plateau — not the processed kind but the whole leaf, which gave the tea a colour like dark amber and a warmth that you could feel moving all the way down. She added the spices: dried ginger, a small piece of clove, a scraping of something from a jar that Baba's mother had given them and which had no official name but which meant warmth in every version of every language.

She put the cup in Bello's hands. He took it.

Neither of them said anything immediately. The fire said what was needed, and the warmth from the cup moving into his cold hands said the rest.

Baba sat in the corner where the staff now rested against the wall, the mud of the plateau still damp on its lower half. He looked at it for a moment. Then he looked at both of his children.

"A staff," he said, "is not a crown."

They both looked at him. He was not looking at either of them specifically — he was looking at some point between them, the place where the argument had lived.

"It is not for one person to hold over another. A crown says: I am above you. A staff says: the ground is uneven and I need help standing on it." He paused. His hands rested on his knees. "Today, you both fell. And one of you lifted the other. And then both of you carried what was too heavy to carry alone." He was quiet for a moment. "That is what a staff is for. Not the wood. The idea in the wood."

Bello looked at the staff. He thought about the weight of it in Zara's hand when she brought it up the slope. Just holding it out. No speech. No I told you so.

He looked at his sister across the fire.

She was looking back at him. The yellow hat was off — it sat on her knee, damp and slightly misshapen. Her face was tired and warm and carrying the specific expression of someone who has come through a hard thing and arrived on the other side of it and is not yet entirely sure what to do with the fact of having arrived.

He thought about the calf. About the way they had moved through the fog — three legs between them, one direction, the wind on her cheek.

"Thank you," he said.

It was only two words. But it was the most honest thing he had said all day, and they both knew it, and Baba heard it from the corner, and the fire heard it, and outside in the pen, in the warmth of the herd, Cloud leaned against his mother and breathed.

On the Mambilla Plateau, the night comes fast and cold and complete. The Cloud-Swallower retreats to the peaks and leaves the stars very bright and very close, the way stars are only at high altitude, as if you could reach one if you stood on tiptoe.

Inside the farmhouse, the two children sat together by the hearth with their cups and their warmth and the particular peace that comes not from the absence of difficulty but from having been through it together. The staff leaned in the corner. The fire burned. The plateau outside was dark and still and very old.

And in the pen, moving carefully on four uncertain legs that were learning, day by day, to find their own footing, the white calf called Cloud pressed close to the warmth of the herd and breathed the cold mountain air and was home.
