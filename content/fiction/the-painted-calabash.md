## Chapter One: The Boy with the Fast Feet

There was a boy in Kano who ran everywhere.

His name was Musa, and he was seven years old, and his feet had not learned the meaning of slow. When his mother sent him to the market for tomatoes, he ran. When the call to prayer rang out from the tall minaret at the end of the road, he ran. When there was absolutely nothing at all to run toward, he ran anyway — just for the pleasure of feeling his legs go fast, and the warm red dust of the lane puffing up between his bare toes like little clouds that he made and left behind.

The red dust of Kano was one of the softest things Musa knew. It was not like the hard-packed earth at the edge of the road, where the donkey carts had pressed it flat and smooth. It was fine and loose and warm, almost like the flour his mother sifted for masa cakes, and it rose around his ankles in a gentle

*whoooosh*

that he loved very much. Sometimes, when he was running, he would stop suddenly — stop dead, all at once — just to watch the little dust storm he had made drift slowly back to earth. It settled like a sigh. Like the dust was happy to go to sleep again.

But today, Musa was not stopping for anyone or anything.

Today was three days before the Durbar.

The Durbar festival came only once a year, and when it came, it turned the whole city of Kano into something that was already a dream. Every street was awake and busy. The air was different — sharper, louder, more alive. The men were out in their compounds polishing leather saddles until the brown leather caught the light like bronze. The women were spreading their best wrappers over low fences to air out, the deep indigo and brilliant orange and dark green cloth hanging like banners from a palace. Old grandmothers were bringing out calabash bowls they only used once a year, setting them in the sun to dry and warm.

Children were running.

Well. Most children were running. Musa was running the most.

He burst through the gate of the family compound with his arms wide and his chin lifted, and he went down the dusty lane like a small fast wind. The chickens — three fat hens who spent their days walking importantly around the lane as if they owned it — exploded outward in a rush of wings and offended squawking.

“Ha! Too slow!” Musa shouted over his shoulder.

The chickens said nothing. They never did. But every single time, Musa counted himself the winner.

The lane smelled of something that made his feet slow just a little, even though the rest of him wanted to go faster. Suya.

The suya sellers were setting up their small fires at the edge of the road, the way they always did when the city was getting ready for something good. Strips of spiced meat — beef, threaded onto thin sticks, coated in a paste of groundnuts and ginger and pepper — were already laid over the coals. The fat dripped down and made the fire spit and hiss. The smoke rose in thin grey curls and carried the smell of pepper and charred meat and sweetness all the way to the top of the lane and into the nose of every person on the street.

Musa stopped.

He stood very still for exactly five seconds, his face pointing toward the nearest suya fire, his eyes closed, his whole body just smelling. His mouth filled with water. His tummy said: yes. Please. Now.

Then his eyes opened and his feet were moving again.

The suya sellers laughed as he went past.

“Musa! Musa, slow down before you run straight into the sky!” called old Alhaji Usman from the low wooden bench in front of his gate. He said this every time Musa went past, and he always laughed when he said it.

Musa grinned over his shoulder. His legs did not slow.

Near the big neem tree at the junction, children were playing a game of catch with a mango someone had found. A boy named Dauda threw it hard. A girl named Fatima caught it one-handed and spun on her heel with a yelp of triumph. Three smaller children screamed and ran in the wrong direction for no reason.

Musa weaved between all of them like a fish going through river reeds. Too quick to be grabbed.

“Race me!” Dauda shouted.

Musa was already gone.

He reached the wooden gate post of his own compound — his finish line, always his finish line, as decided by himself and agreed with by no one else — and he slapped it with his open palm.

SMACK.

He stopped. His chest moved in and out, in and out, hard and fast. The red dust had climbed all the way up the front of his legs and covered both his hands. He was painted red from the knees down.

He looked at his feet. They were completely red. They were the most beautiful colour he had seen all day.

He sat down right there in the lane and looked at his own feet for a moment, breathing.

Three days until the Durbar.

He was the happiest boy in the whole of Kano, and possibly in the whole of the world.

He went inside to find Kaka.

## Chapter Two: Kaka’s Masterpiece

Kaka was sitting on a woven mat in the inner courtyard, and she was doing something so careful that Musa stopped the moment he saw her.

He stopped so suddenly that his feet skidded on the smooth earth floor of the compound. He pressed his hand over his mouth so he would not make a noise. He was not sure why he did not want to make a noise. He just knew, looking at her, that being loud right now would be wrong.

She was painting.

On the mat in front of her sat the biggest calabash Musa had ever seen. It was the size of a large cooking pot, perfectly round, its outside surface pale and dry and smooth as paper. In Kaka’s hand was a thin reed, and dipped onto the end of that reed was a bright red dye. She was drawing a pattern on the skin of the calabash, and the pattern moved in careful curves and sharp corners, repeating itself around the bowl the way a song repeats its chorus — the same shape coming back each time, but never exactly the same, always a little different, a little alive.

Musa crept closer.

He crouched down and put his chin on the edge of the mat and looked.

Red like the coals in a fire. Yellow like the afternoon sun when it went fat and low over the city. Black like the thick shadow underneath the mango tree at the hottest part of the day. The three colours worked together in a pattern that seemed too perfect for a reed and a hand to have made. There were diamonds inside diamonds. Lines that curved one way, then turned sharp and went another. And small dots, each one placed exactly, spaced exactly, so that the whole thing looked like it had not been made but had always been there, waiting to be uncovered.

Kaka had been making this for three days.

Three whole days of sitting still with a thin reed.

Musa could not sit still for three minutes.

“Kaka,” he whispered. “It is beautiful.”

Kaka smiled, but she did not look up. This was one of the things Musa loved most about his grandmother. When she was working, she was truly working. She did not stop for anything that was not important.

“Thank you, my boy,” she said. Her voice was low and warm and steady, the way the evening sounds when everything is in its right place.

Musa looked at the pattern again. He looked at the way the red lines curved around the roundness of the bowl and disappeared at the back and then came around again at the front, so that every part of the calabash belonged to the whole of it.

“What is it for?” he asked.

Kaka dipped the reed very gently in the little cup of dye. “I will fill it with kola nuts and carry it to the start of the Durbar ceremony. It will be presented at the Emir’s parade.” She touched the reed to the calabash in one small, careful stroke. “It is an old tradition in this family. My own mother made one. Her mother made one before her. And now I make mine.”

Musa was quiet for a moment.

He had not thought about that before. He had not thought about the calabash being something that went backward in time as well as forward. He looked at it with different eyes now. Somewhere inside the painted pattern were his great-grandmother’s hands, and hands before those hands, all the way back to women whose names he did not know but who had sat just like this on just such a mat, with a reed and a bowl and a very quiet kind of patience.

He reached out to touch the curve of it.

“Do not touch it.” Kaka’s voice was still gentle, but it was very firm. Like a wall that was covered in soft cloth but was still a wall. “The dye is wet, Musa. One smear and three days of work is nothing.”

Musa pulled his hand back fast.

Kaka tapped the side of the calabash with one knuckle.

Tock. Tock.

The sound was hollow and dry and woody, like knocking on a small door that led somewhere dark and secret.

“Hear that?” Kaka said. “Old and strong and hollow. A calabash this dry and well-kept is very hard, but it is also brittle. If you drop it, it does not bend. It breaks. And once it breaks, it is gone.” She looked up at Musa then, for the first time. Her eyes were the colour of dark earth after rain — deep and serious and warm at the same time. “Outside is for running,” she said. “Inside the house is only for walking. Do you understand me, Musa?”

Musa looked at the calabash. He looked at the pattern. He thought about all the grandmothers who had made one, going all the way back.

“Yes, Kaka,” he said. “I understand.”

He meant it completely.

He meant it with every part of himself, right down to his fast feet.

He just did not know yet that meaning something and remembering it are sometimes two different things.

## Chapter Three: The Big Crash

The next afternoon was the hottest afternoon there had ever been.

At least, that was how it felt.

The sun had climbed straight to the top of the sky and was sitting there without moving, pouring heat down on Kano like water poured from a very large pot. The earth was hot. The compound walls were hot. Even the air was hot, thick and still and pressing, like wearing a warm blanket you could not take off.

Nobody wanted to go outside.

Even the chickens had given up. They were sitting in a row in the narrow shadow beside the compound wall, their wings slightly open, doing nothing. They had stopped being important. The heat had defeated them completely.

Musa was inside with his two cousins.

Bilal was eight years old and very good at thinking of things to do indoors. Sadiya was five years old and not very good at most things yet, but she tried extremely hard, which more than made up for it.

“Hide-and-seek,” Bilal said.

Musa looked toward the hallway. “Inside?”

“It is too hot outside.” Bilal said this with the confident finality of someone who had already decided and was only announcing it, not asking.

Musa thought about the calabash on its table in the room at the end of the hall. He thought about Kaka’s words.

Inside the house is only for walking.

He thought about it.

Then Bilal pressed his face to the wall and began to count, and Sadiya ran giggling toward the kitchen, and the game had already started.

“One, two, three—”

Musa moved down the hallway, looking for a hiding place. The tall clay water pot in the corner was too narrow. The big wooden chest was locked. Behind the door of the storage room was perfect. He slipped behind it and pressed himself flat against the wall and held his breath.

He heard Bilal find Sadiya almost immediately. She had gone to the kitchen. She always went to the kitchen. Bilal knew this. Everyone knew this. But Sadiya was five, and at five years old, a place feels like a good hiding place if you like it, regardless of whether it actually works.

Now it was Sadiya’s turn to count.

Sadiya counted very slowly. She was still learning her numbers past fifteen.

“One... two... four...”

Musa waited. He could hear her small footsteps coming down the hallway. Closer. Closer.

They stopped right outside the storage room door.

She had heard him. She must have heard him breathing. She was going to open the door and find him and shout “Got you!” and he would be out.

And something happened.

It was not a decision. It was not something Musa thought about. It was something that happened below thinking, in the part of a seven-year-old boy that is made entirely of fast feet and the need to win.

He ran.

He burst out from behind the door and his feet hit the smooth floor and he was already going fast, full speed, arms pumping, chin up, the way he ran in the lane —

His left foot hit the corner of the woven mat in the hallway.

The mat slid.

His arms flew out.

His right hand caught the corner of the table.

The table rocked.

CRACK.

The sound was the worst sound Musa had ever heard in his life.

It was not a soft sound. It was not a sound that left any room for hope. It was dry and sharp and final — the sound of something old and strong being split completely in two, and when a thing breaks like that, it does not unbreak. It is simply done.

Musa stood very still.

He looked down.

The calabash lay on the floor in two pieces. Two perfect half-circles, their painted faces turned upward, the red and yellow and black pattern staring at him from the ground. The line where they had broken ran straight through the middle of the biggest diamond, splitting it exactly in half.

The house was silent.

The silence was enormous. It pressed against Musa’s ears like something physical. He had never noticed before how much sound a house made when nothing was happening — the distant creak of a wall, the faint movement of air through the high window, the soft shuffle of the chickens outside.

And then he heard his heart.

*Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Very loud. Very fast. Knocking against the inside of his chest like something that wanted to get out.

He looked toward the kitchen. No sound from Kaka. He looked toward Sadiya. Sadiya was standing in the hallway with her hand over her mouth and her eyes very round.

“Go,” Musa whispered to her urgently.

Sadiya turned and walked away quickly, the way a person walks when they want very much not to be involved in something.

Musa stood alone over the two halves of his grandmother’s masterpiece.

His heart said: *thump thump thump*.

His legs felt like they were made of warm water.

And at the back of his mind, very small and very fast, a thought arrived.

Fix it.

## Chapter Four: The Sticky Secret

Musa listened for Kaka.

He could hear her sandals moving in the back of the house, near the kitchen. She was humming to herself — the low, easy hum of someone who is doing ordinary work on an ordinary afternoon and does not know that anything has changed.

She had not heard the crack.

Musa let out a long, shaky breath.

He had to fix it now. He had to fix it before the humming stopped, before the sandals turned and came toward the hallway. He had maybe five minutes. Maybe less.

He thought of the rubber tree.

It stood at the back corner of the compound yard, old and dark-barked and enormous, its roots coming up out of the ground in thick, twisting ropes. Every child in the compound knew about it. It was the stickiest tree in the world. If you scratched its bark with a stick, or broke off a small branch, thick white sap came oozing out — slowly at first, just a bead, and then more, thicker, like the tree was bleeding something white and heavy.

The sap stuck to everything.

Bilal had once got it on three fingers and could not separate them for an entire afternoon. He had walked around with his hand held out, his fingers stuck together, saying “Don’t touch me” to everyone he passed. It had made everyone laugh and made Bilal very unhappy.

If it could stick three fingers together, it could stick two pieces of calabash.

Musa moved.

He was out the back door and across the hot yard and at the base of the rubber tree in less time than it took to say his own name. He found a small sharp stone and dragged it along the bark in the spot he remembered — the low spot, near the biggest root. The bark opened. A bead of white sap appeared. Then another. Then a thin white trickle that thickened as he watched.

Musa held his fingers under it.

The sap landed on his fingertips and immediately began to grip.

It was warm. It was incredibly sticky — stickier than honey, stickier than any glue he had ever touched. It did not feel like a liquid. It felt like something alive that had decided to hold on. When he pressed his fingers together and pulled them apart, long thin threads of white stretched between them before finally snapping.

He scooped up as much as his fingers could carry and ran back inside.

He knelt on the floor over the two broken halves.

He spread the sap along the broken edge of the larger piece with his thumb, pressing it into the rough gap. The sap was already sticking to him, trying to pull his fingers down to the wood, but he kept working. He ran the sap along the whole line of the break, all the way around.

Then he picked up the smaller half and pressed the two pieces together.

Squee-squee-squee.

The sound the wood made was a rubbery, slightly horrible sound — the sound of two dry surfaces being pushed together with something wet and gluey between them. It was not a good sound. But the two halves held.

Musa pressed hard. He pressed with his thumbs on both sides, his fingers overlapping around the bowl, squeezing. He held it for a long moment, perfectly still. He could feel the sap hardening already — going from liquid to thick to something papery and tight.

He let go carefully.

The calabash stayed whole.

He let out another breath.

He tilted the calabash and looked at the crack. It was still there. It was not invisible. From close up, in good light, you could see the thin dark line running across the yellow diamond, and you could see the faint white edge of the dried sap if you knew to look.

But from far away, in the dim light of the room, it was much harder to see.

He stood up. He turned the calabash slowly until the cracked side faced away from the room, toward the back wall. He stepped back three paces. He tilted his head one way, then the other.

You could not tell.

He wiped his forehead with the back of his hand and left a long white smear of dried sap across his face. He did not notice.

His hands were a disaster. The sap had dried on his fingers in a thin, papery, impossible shell. He rubbed his fingers together. The shell crinkled but did not come off. It stretched and squeaked between his finger pads like a second skin made of glue.

He would wash them later.

He picked up the calabash one more time and placed it back on the table, crack-side to the wall. He stepped back. He nodded once, to nobody.

He went and sat in the corner of the room, his back against the wall, his sticky fingers spread open on his knees.

Outside, the afternoon was still impossibly hot. The chickens had not moved from their line of shade. The city of Kano was going about its business.

And Musa sat in the corner and felt, for the first time, something new arrive in his tummy.

It was not big. Not yet. Just the first small heaviness of something settling in — a round, blunt, quiet weight sitting right behind his belly button.

He told himself it was nothing.

He pulled at the dried sap on his fingers.

It was not nothing.

## Chapter Five: A Tummy Full of Stones

Evening came in slowly.

The heat did not go all at once. It let go of the day the way a fist unclenches, finger by finger — the sky going from hard white to pale gold to a deep, heavy orange that sat over the rooftops like a warm hand being lifted away. Bats appeared above the compound walls, small and quick and stitching themselves silently back and forth across the last of the light.

Musa sat in the doorway of the inner room and watched them.

He had been very quiet all afternoon. He had not run. He had not played. He had sat in his corner for a while, then moved to the doorway, then sat on the low step outside for a while, then come back in. He had not known what to do with himself. His body, which usually knew exactly what to do at every moment, had no idea today.

He heard Kaka’s sandals on the floor behind him.

She put her hand on the top of his head — her hand was warm and slightly rough from years of work, and the weight of it on his head was the most familiar feeling in the world.

“You have been so quiet all afternoon, my boy,” she said. “That is not like you.”

“I am just tired,” Musa said.

He was not tired. He did not feel anything like tired. He felt tight and watchful, like a bird sitting on a very narrow branch.

“A good, calm boy,” Kaka said, and she patted his head once. “I am proud of you.”

She walked back into the room, and Musa stared at the bats.

A good boy.

The words hit him somewhere in the middle. He pressed his lips together very hard. He watched a bat do a sudden sharp turn in the air and disappear over the wall.

That was when the stone became real.

Before, it had just been a heaviness. A little discomfort, like wearing a shirt that was slightly too tight. But when Kaka said those two words — good boy — the stone became specific and solid and round, and it sat right behind his belly button with no intention of ever leaving.

He was not a good boy.

He was a boy who had run inside when he was told not to, and broken something precious, and hidden the pieces with tree sap, and turned the evidence to face the wall.

The bats flew. The sky went dark.

The smell came before the sound.

Kaka was making Tuwo Shinkafa.

This was Musa’s favourite meal in the entire world. He had said this many times and he meant it every time. The smooth white rice mash — thick and soft, not quite solid, not quite liquid, somewhere perfect in between — that you rolled between your fingers into small balls and dipped into the soup. And the soup was Kaka’s soup, which was different from everyone else’s soup, which had in it some spice from a small brown jar that Kaka kept at the very back of the highest shelf and would not share the name of with anyone, not even Musa’s mother. The smell of it was the smell of being home, being safe, being exactly where you were supposed to be.

On any other evening, the smell would have brought Musa to the kitchen before he was called.

Tonight he went when he was told to and sat down and looked at the bowl.

The tuwo was smooth and white. The soup was deep brown-red, with little rings of oil shimmering on the surface that caught the lamplight and turned it to gold. It smelled exactly like it always smelled. It was exactly as good as it always was.

He took a small piece of tuwo and rolled it between his fingers and dipped it in the soup and put it in his mouth.

He chewed.

It tasted like warm air. Like the idea of food rather than food itself. The flavour was somewhere far away, behind a wall, and his tongue could not quite reach it. He swallowed. The ball of tuwo went down slowly and found the stone in his tummy and sat on top of it.

“You are picking like a sick bird,” Kaka said, watching him. Not unkindly. Just noticing.

“My tummy is already full, Kaka.”

Kaka looked at him. She had the kind of eyes that read faces the way hands read cloth — by touch, by feel, not by looking. Musa looked at his bowl.

“Full already? You ran all morning and you are full?”

“Yes, Kaka.”

She did not push him further. She let him leave the food. She said the evening prayer and called Musa to say his beside her, and they knelt together in the warm lamplight, and Musa said each word of the prayer correctly.

But the stone was there the whole time. It was there through the prayer. It was there when he lay down on his mat. It was there when his mother kissed his forehead. It was still there when the lamp was put out and the room went dark.

The smell of Kaka’s soup still drifted from the kitchen.

It just did not help at all.

## Chapter Six: The Sweet Dates Lose Their Taste

The next morning was the morning before the Durbar.

From the first call to prayer, before the sun had even fully arrived, Kano was already loud. Musa could hear it from his mat. Voices calling over compound walls. The sharp ring of a blacksmith’s hammer somewhere down the road. The deep, distant pulse of a drum being tested — one heavy beat, silence, another, silence, like someone checking that the world was still working before the big day began.

He lay on his mat and listened.

Usually these sounds on the day before the Durbar would have had him off his mat and outside before anyone could blink. Usually he would have been running down to the junction to see what was happening before his mother had finished tying her wrapper.

This morning, he lay still.

He ate his breakfast without tasting it. He helped Bilal carry water from the big pot in the courtyard to the kitchen without being asked, which made his mother look at him with a pleased but slightly confused face. He swept the front step of the compound. He straightened the mats.

He was not being good. He was being busy. There is a difference. Being good comes from the inside. Being busy is just something to do with your hands when the inside of you is not comfortable.

Kaka was in the inner room, near the calabash table. She was sorting through her good cloth, holding pieces up to the light to decide which wrapper to wear tomorrow. Each time her hands moved near the table, Musa’s whole body became a rope pulled very tight.

Then, late in the morning, Uncle Ibrahim arrived.

Uncle Ibrahim was large and warm and loud, and his laugh came from somewhere below his ribs and shook his whole body when it arrived. He drove a red pickup truck that backfired going over bumps, which made small children jump and then laugh. He brought something with him whenever he came, because this was simply his way.

Today he carried a wide, flat basket covered with a cloth.

He pulled the cloth off with a little ceremony, the way a market seller reveals something he knows you will want.

Dates.

Not just any dates. These were the big Northern dates, the kind that only arrived in the market a few weeks each year, the ones that were almost the size of a thumb and had gone so perfectly ripe that the skin had darkened and gone slightly sticky on the outside. They smelled of honey and of something warm and floral underneath, like a flower made of sugar. These were Musa’s absolutely most favourite thing in the world to eat. He had said so many times. Everyone in the family knew.

“Musa! Come and greet your uncle and take your dates,” his mother called.

Musa came. He greeted his uncle properly. Uncle Ibrahim ruffled his hair and put three of the biggest dates directly into his palm.

“There, there, my fast boy,” Uncle Ibrahim said. “Eat before I change my mind!” He laughed his whole-body laugh and went to find Kaka to greet her.

Musa sat down with his three dates in his hand.

He looked at them. They were perfect. Round and dark and gleaming, and they smelled of everything wonderful, and his mouth remembered them even though he had not eaten one yet.

He looked up.

Kaka had come back into the inner room. She was not near the calabash table, but she was in the room. She was talking to Uncle Ibrahim, gesturing with her hands the way she did when she was telling a story that needed gesturing. Her back was to the table.

Musa watched her.

Then she stopped gesturing and turned to reach for something on the shelf near the window, and for one second she was facing the table, and Musa’s whole chest pulled tight.

She did not look at the calabash. She found what she wanted on the shelf. She turned back to Uncle Ibrahim.

Musa breathed.

He looked down at his hand. He had squeezed the dates so hard while he was watching that his fingers had pressed deep into one of them and dark sticky juice was running down his wrist. He held a crumpled, squashed thing that was still technically a date but only barely.

He put one in his mouth.

He chewed it.

It tasted like nothing. Like warm sweetness that had lost its meaning. Like sugar with no happiness inside it. He chewed and swallowed and tried again with the second one and got the same result.

He put the third one in his pocket.

Uncle Ibrahim’s laugh floated out from the inner room. “What is wrong with the boy?” he called. “He has dates and a long face! Tomorrow is Durbar!”

Musa smiled. He pulled the smile up deliberately, the way you pull a heavy bucket up from a well. It came. It was not his real smile, but it would do.

“Yes, Uncle,” he said.

He went and sat against the compound wall in a thin strip of shade. The red dust was warm against the backs of his thighs. He looked at the ground between his feet.

One more afternoon. One more night.

The stone in his tummy had not gotten any lighter. If anything, it was bigger today than it had been yesterday. Each hour it sat there, it seemed to grow a little, the way a wet clay ball gets heavier as it dries.

He reached into his pocket and looked at the last date.

He put it back.

## Chapter Seven: The Crickets Are Tattletales

Bedtime. The Durbar was tomorrow.

Musa lay on his sleeping mat with his eyes open and the ceiling above him going slowly darker as the lamplight moved from the room and left. He could hear his parents behind the curtain, talking quietly, the low comfortable sound of two people saying ordinary things to each other at the end of an ordinary day.

His day had not been ordinary.

His was the only mat in the room that was not filled with someone sleeping. Bilal was already asleep — you could tell because his breathing had slowed and flattened and had a little snore in it, the kind of snore that only happens when a person is completely comfortable and has nothing worrying them at all. Sadiya, across the room, was breathing the deep, even breath of someone who had run very hard during the day and had nothing in their conscience and was sleeping like a stone dropped into still water.

Musa lay on his mat and stared at the ceiling and felt the stone in his tummy and tried to find a comfortable position.

He turned on his side. The mat felt different tonight. The same mat he had slept on for three years, but tonight the plaited grass scratched at the side of his knee and the back of his arm in a way he had never noticed before. He turned to the other side. The other side was the same.

He pushed his wrapper down. He was hot. He pushed it further. Still hot.

He lay flat on his back again.

Through the high window on the far wall, he could see a small rectangle of night sky with three stars in it. He looked at the stars. The stars were very still and very far away, and they did not look like they had opinions about anything.

Outside, in the tall dry grass beside the compound wall, the crickets had begun.

*Chirp-chirp. Chirp-chirp.*

Musa knew the crickets. He had been listening to them every night of his life. They were the sound of night in Kano, as familiar as the call to prayer, as comfortable as the smell of Kaka’s cooking. On every other night of his life, the crickets had been like a song that helped him fall asleep.

Tonight they were not a song.

He lay still and listened, and the more he listened, the more the sound shifted in his ears. It lost its ordinary meaning and grew a new one, the way a word loses its meaning if you say it too many times and becomes just a sound. The chirp-chirp was still a chirp-chirp. But inside his ears, the two syllables had become something else.

He broke it. He broke it. He broke it.

Musa pressed his hands flat over his ears.

The sound stopped. Silence.

He let his hands slip.

The crickets came back.

He broke it. He broke it.

He turned over hard and pressed his face into his wrapper and lay there breathing the warm cotton smell and tried to think about the horses. He was good at thinking about the horses when he needed to think about something else. The great Durbar horses with their embroidered saddle cloths and their high-held heads and the way they moved in the parade — proud and powerful and perfectly trained, each step placed exactly where the rider asked it. He thought about the thunder of their hooves on the packed earth. He thought about the cavalry turning toward the Emir’s grandstand, the dust rising in great red clouds —

What if Kaka checks the calabash before morning?

The thought arrived out of nowhere and landed hard.

What if she wakes up early — she always woke before everyone, Kaka, always — and goes to the storage room and sees the crack? What if the sap gives way in the night and the bowl falls apart on the table all by itself, and she hears the crash and comes in and finds it?

His eyes were open again.

The horses were gone.

He sat up on his mat. The room was dark and warm and full of the sound of other people sleeping.

He thought about going to Kaka now. About walking to her mat and kneeling down and putting his hand on her arm and saying: Kaka. Wake up. I need to tell you something.

He imagined it in great detail. He imagined the way her eyes would open slowly and then quickly, the way they did when something was wrong. He imagined her face when he told her — the eyebrows lifting, the mouth going quiet, the look that was not anger but was something close enough that a seven-year-old could not tell the difference.

He lay back down.

He could not do it. Not in the dark. Not now. Not tonight, the night before the Durbar, when everyone was meant to be happy and sleeping well.

Tomorrow, he told himself. After the festival. When everything is good and the day is over and Kaka has had her celebration and the kola nuts have been presented and the Emir has ridden past and the drums have drummed and the trumpets have soared — then he would tell her. Then it would be easier.

He closed his eyes.

The crickets chirped.

He pulled the wrapper up to his chin and stared at the darkness behind his eyelids.

He did not sleep for a very long time.

## Chapter Eight: The Trumpets Sound

Durbar morning arrived through the window before Musa’s eyes opened.

It came in as sound first. Not just any sound. The full, deep, resonant boom of the gangan drums — not the single testing beats he had been hearing all week from practice sessions down the road, but the real thing, all of them at once, played by many hands together. It was so big it was not just a sound you heard. It entered through the soles of your feet and moved up through your legs and into your chest and sat there, beating alongside your own heartbeat, saying:

BOOM. BOOM-boom-boom. BOOM.

Musa’s eyes opened.

Then the kakaki.

The long silver trumpets of Kano only spoke on the greatest days of the year. They had a voice like nothing else in the world — deep and wide and ancient, starting low and rising, a sound that seemed to come from somewhere much older than any living person, from somewhere deep in the long story of the city itself. It was the sound of Kano saying: I am still here. I am still myself. I have always been here.

Even the walls seemed to listen when the kakaki sounded.

Bilal was sitting up on his mat with his eyes wide and his wrapper around his shoulders. “Durbar day! Musa! It is Durbar morning!” He sounded like he was announcing something Musa did not already know.

Musa sat up.

Through the high window, the sky was the specific bright gold of Kano at very early morning — the sun just clearing the tops of the compounds, the air not yet hot, everything clean and sharp and full of promise. He could hear horses. Real horses, close by, moving with the particular clip-clip of hooves on hard earth, and the jingle of the silver bits in their mouths, and once a short high neigh from an animal that was dressed in its best saddle cloth and knew that today was a day for being magnificent.

For one full, perfect moment, Musa forgot.

He forgot the calabash. He forgot the tree sap on his fingers. He forgot three days of a stone in his tummy and dates that tasted like nothing and cricket sounds in the dark. He was just a seven-year-old boy on Durbar morning in Kano, and the world was loud and golden and full of drums, and he was in it.

He swung his legs off the mat.

He stood up.

Kaka was in the doorway.

She was dressed in her finest wrapper — deep blue with gold thread woven through it in thin lines, so that when she moved, the whole cloth caught the light and shifted like water. Her head wrap was tied in a knot that took time and practice to make. She had her best leather sandals on. She was carrying the faint scent of the good perfume she kept in the small bottle on the high shelf, the one she used only for festivals and celebrations.

She looked like a queen. Not the queens in stories, with crowns and thrones. A real queen. The kind that was made of patience and long work and love carried carefully for a very long time.

She smiled at Musa, wide and warm.

“Good morning, my fast boy! Can you hear the city?”

“Yes, Kaka.” The smile came to Musa’s face on its own, because the sight of her put it there.

“Good.” She clapped her hands once, the quick sharp clap she made when she was pleased and ready. “Now. There is one thing to do before we go.” She looked at Musa directly, and she was still smiling, and she said: “Go carefully and bring me my calabash. I want to wash it and set the kola nuts inside before we leave. Go carefully now — carry it with both hands.”

She turned and went back down the hallway toward the kitchen.

Musa sat on the edge of his mat.

The drums were still booming. The kakaki were still lifting into the golden air. Somewhere close, a horse moved and its silver bridle rang.

Musa’s legs had become heavy. Not just heavy. They felt like they had been filled with wet sand since the last time he used them. He stood up. He sat back down. He stood up again.

He could tell Kaka now. Right now. This second, before he moved toward the room. He could call her back and say: Kaka. Wait. There is something I need to tell you first.

But his mouth did not open.

His feet moved instead.

Down the hallway. Past the water pot. Past the wooden chest. One step at a time, as slow as he had ever walked in his life — Musa, who ran everywhere, who had never met a surface he did not want to cross at full speed, walking now as if each step was costing him something.

He reached the room.

The calabash was on its table, turned toward the wall, exactly as he had left it.

He stood in front of it.

His hands were shaking.

## Chapter Nine: The Broken Pieces

Musa stood in front of the table and looked at the calabash.

The morning light came through the high window in a clean, straight stripe across the floor. The light was bright and clear and honest, the way early morning light is before the heat has changed it. It fell across the table and across the back of the calabash and lit up the section of painted pattern that faced outward, the red and yellow and black still vivid and sharp.

The crack was on the side facing the wall. He could not see it. But he knew it was there.

He had known it was there every moment of the last three days.

He put both hands on the bowl the way Kaka had asked. His hands were shaking in a way he could not stop, a fine, constant tremble. He tried to make them stop. He told them to stop. They did not stop.

He tried to lift the bowl.

His fingers would not close properly. He gripped the sides and his grip kept loosening, as if some part of him below his thinking — some honest, knowing part — was refusing to bring the bowl to Kaka and start the next thing happening. He tried again. He tightened his fingers. He got his hands around the widest part of the bowl.

Under his left thumb, he felt the crack.

A thin raised line. The faint ridge where the tree sap had dried between the two broken edges. He pressed it gently with his thumb. The two halves moved. Very slightly — just a little give, the tiny softness of something that was held together but not sealed, glued but not whole.

The sap had dried and shrunk and was not the same sap it had been two days ago.

He was still standing there with his hands on the bowl and the stone enormous in his tummy and his whole body shaking when he heard Kaka’s voice right behind him.

“Let me help you, little one. It is heavy for small hands.”

Musa spun around.

Kaka was in the doorway, still in her blue and gold, holding out both hands toward the calabash with the easy confidence of someone who has lifted this object many times and does not expect a problem.

“I—” Musa said.

One syllable. That was all he got.

Because Kaka had already stepped forward, and her hands were already on the calabash, and she was already lifting it from the table with the same unhurried and certain grip she used for everything.

She turned it toward herself to look at the pattern.

For one last second, the morning was still.

Then the dried sap, which had held for two days and two nights and was now old and brittle and thin and completely finished, let go all at once, cleanly, the way a thing breaks when it was always going to break and had only been waiting for the right moment.

CRACK.

The two halves of the calabash split apart and fell from Kaka’s hands.

CLATTER. CLATTER.

They hit the hard earth floor and bounced once each and came to rest on either side of her feet. The painted face of the calabash stared up from the floor in two halves — half a diamond here, half a diamond there, the red and yellow and black pattern split cleanly down the middle, exactly as it had been three days ago before the sap had hidden the truth.

Silence.

The drums were still booming outside. The kakaki were still playing. The whole city of Kano was awake and celebrating on the other side of the compound wall.

In this room, there was no sound at all.

Kaka looked at the two pieces. She looked at the broken edges. She saw the dried white tree sap on both sides, crumbled now, papery, old. She looked at it for a long moment. Then she looked at Musa.

And Musa’s face went into his hands.

He did not plan it. He did not decide it. His face just went there, the way it did when something was too big for the front of his face to hold, and then the tears came, and they were not polite tears. They were the big, shaking, whole-body kind that take you completely over and will not be stopped or made smaller or sent somewhere quieter.

He stood with his face in his palms and his shoulders jumping up and down and his breath coming in uneven, noisy gasps.

He waited.

He waited for the voice to go sharp and high and loud. He waited for the question to crack out like the calabash had cracked: Who did this? Why? How could you?

He waited for the look that was worse than any shouting, the look that said: I trusted you.

He waited.

The drums played. The city celebrated.

Two warm arms came around him.

## Chapter Ten: Lighter Than a Feather

The arms came around him from behind and pulled him in, and then Kaka was on her knees on the floor in her best blue and gold wrapper — the one that took twenty minutes to arrange, the one she only wore once a year — kneeling on the hard earth floor of the room and holding Musa against her in the space between her shoulder and her neck. The same space he had fitted in when he was small enough to be carried. He still fitted.

She smelled of the soap she used in the mornings and the faint sweetness of the festival perfume and, underneath both, just the particular smell of Kaka, which was not like any other smell in the world and which Musa would have been able to find in the dark.

She held him, and he cried.

He cried harder, actually, now that the arms were there. He had not meant to do that. He had expected to stop crying when something happened, but the something that had happened was this — not shouting, not the sharp voice, but arms and warmth — and his tears had no idea what to do with that, so they kept coming for a good while longer.

Kaka held him and did not speak.

She let him cry until the crying was finished. She had always done this. She never rushed his feelings. She treated feelings the way she treated dye on the calabash: you have to let it set in its own time, or it will not take properly.

When the shaking had stopped and the gasping had settled into the uneven, hitching breath of someone who has finished crying but is still coming back to themselves, Kaka put her hands on either side of his face.

She lifted it up.

He tried to turn away. His eyes were swollen and his face was a mess and he did not want to be looked at.

“No,” she said quietly. “Look at me, Musa.”

He looked at her.

Her face was serious, yes. But it was not the serious he had been afraid of for three days. It was a larger kind — the kind that contains both sadness and love at the same time and does not ask either one to leave to make room for the other.

“Tell me all of it,” she said.

So he did.

He told her about the hide-and-seek and the counting and the storage room. He told her about hearing Sadiya’s footsteps and the way his feet had moved before he had thought about it. He told her about the mat and the table and the terrible crack and the silence and the sound of his own heartbeat. He told her about the rubber tree and the sap and how it had stuck to his fingers. He told her about pressing the two halves together and the squeaky sound they made. He told her about turning the calabash to face the wall. He told her about the dinner he could not eat and the prayer he said without meaning it. He told her about the dates that tasted like sand. He told her about the crickets.

He told her all of it.

And with each piece he told, something happened in his tummy.

The stone moved.

It shifted, slowly at first, then more, the way a stuck wheel starts to turn when you find the right place to push. By the time he reached the part about the crickets, it had lost most of its weight. When he got to the end, when he looked at her and said, very simply, “I am sorry, Kaka” — it was gone.

Not pushed away. Not hidden. Gone the way things go when they are truly finished.

Kaka was quiet for a moment. She looked at the two halves of the calabash on the floor between them. Musa watched her face and saw a real sadness move across it — not an angry sadness, but a quiet one. The sadness of losing something that had taken time and care and love to make. He saw it and he did not look away. He was supposed to see it. It was part of telling the whole truth, and you had to be willing to see what your choices had cost.

Then Kaka looked back at him.

“A calabash is wood, Musa,” she said. “Wood breaks. I will make another one. I will take my time and I will make a better one.” She wiped both sides of his face with the edge of her wrapper. “But I am not sad only about the bowl. I am sad that my sweet grandson carried a heavy, painful thing alone inside himself for three days. Three whole days! All that weight and no one to help you hold it.”

Musa’s chin went wobbly again. He pressed his lips together.

“I was scared you would be angry,” he said.

“I am a little sad,” Kaka said. “Sad about the bowl. Sad that you ran inside when you knew the rule. Those things are true.” She looked at him directly, both hands still on his face. “But angry? Musa. You are seven years old. Seven-year-olds run. God made your legs for running, and sometimes they forget where they are. What I care about — what I always care about — is what you do after. And what you did after was carry the truth until you could not anymore, and then you put it down.” She pulled him close for one more moment. “That is not a bad boy. That is a boy who is learning. And learning is good. Learning is the whole point.”

Musa breathed.

He breathed in as deeply as his lungs would go, all the way to the very bottom. His chest filled up to the top.

He let it out.

His tummy was empty. Not empty the way it is before a meal. Empty the way the sky is after a rain has finished — clean and open and ready.

Kaka stood up and smoothed her wrapper. She looked at the two pieces of calabash on the table where she had placed them, set carefully side by side, not thrown.

“Now,” she said. “We have a Durbar to get to.”

“But the kola nuts —”

“I have a small woven basket. The Emir’s family has received gifts from this family for forty years. A basket instead of a calabash will not trouble anyone.” She held out her hand to Musa. “And when I make the new one — and I will make a new one — you will sit beside me while I paint it. So you know what it costs.”

Musa nodded. “Yes, Kaka. I would like that.”

She squeezed his hand once.

They walked out of the room together. Down the hallway, through the compound, out through the gate and into the morning.

The city of Kano hit them like a wave.

The gangan drums were everywhere, filling the whole sky with their deep rolling sound. The kakaki horns soared over them, long and silver and ancient. There were people in their best clothes filling every street, children running, old men seated in chairs of honour, horses moving in a stately procession with their silver bridles catching the light. The red dust of the city rose in thin clouds everywhere feet touched the earth.

Musa stood in it all and breathed.

He was not running. He was just standing. Standing very still, beside his grandmother, with her hand warm in his, and his chest open, and his tummy light.

Lighter, he thought, than the red dust rising around his feet.

Lighter than the bats over the compound wall.

Lighter, even, than a feather.

He watched the Durbar with clear eyes, and it was the most beautiful thing he had ever seen.

Later that afternoon, his mother put a small plate of dates on the mat in front of him.

He picked one up. He put it in his mouth. He closed his eyes.

It tasted of honey and warmth and sweetness and something almost like flowers.

It tasted exactly like itself.

He ate five.

## Glossary

- **Durbar**: A grand royal festival celebrated in the Hausa and Fulani kingdoms of northern Nigeria, especially in Kano. During the Durbar, horses and their riders parade in beautiful costumes before the Emir as a sign of respect and loyalty. It is one of the most spectacular festivals in all of Nigeria.

- **Emir**: The traditional royal ruler of a Hausa kingdom, like the Emirate of Kano. The Emir is a very important and respected leader, similar to a king. During the Durbar, everything is done in the Emir’s honour.

- **Kakaki**: Long, straight silver trumpets that are played only during very important royal occasions in northern Nigeria. They make a deep, wide, soaring sound that can be heard across the whole city. Kakaki are among the most ancient royal instruments in West Africa.

- **Gangan**: A type of talking drum played in Yoruba and Hausa traditions. It has a deep, resonant boom and is used to announce festivals, celebrations, and important events. The gangan at the Durbar is so loud and powerful that it feels like the ground itself is keeping the beat.

- **Cavalry**: Soldiers or riders who fight or parade on horseback. At the Durbar, the cavalry are the groups of horse riders in beautiful robes who gallop toward the Emir in a thrilling display. The horses and riders train together for a long time to perform perfectly.

- **Minaret**: The tall, thin tower attached to a mosque from which the call to prayer is announced. Musa hears the call to prayer from the minaret at the end of his road every day.

- **Procession**: A long, slow, orderly parade of people or animals moving together for a special purpose or ceremony. The Durbar horses moving through the streets are part of a royal procession.

- **Bridle**: The set of straps and a metal bit that goes around a horse’s head and mouth, used by the rider to guide and control the horse. At the Durbar, the horses’ bridles are decorated with silver that catches the light.

- **Conscience**: The quiet voice inside you that tells you whether something you have done is right or wrong. When Musa could not sleep, it was his conscience keeping him awake, reminding him about the broken calabash.

- **Brittle**: Something that is hard but will snap and break easily if dropped or bent, because it has no flex or softness in it. Old, dry wood is brittle. Kaka warned Musa that the calabash was brittle and could not be dropped.

- **Vivid**: Very bright, strong, and clear — used to describe colours that are almost alive. The red, yellow, and black pattern on the calabash was vivid, meaning the colours were bold and sharp and eye-catching.

- **Resonant**: A deep, rich sound that seems to fill the whole space and linger in the air, like the sound of a large drum or a deep bell. The gangan drums at the Durbar had a resonant sound that entered Musa’s chest.

- **Stately**: Moving in a slow, grand, dignified way that carries importance and respect. The Durbar horses moved in a stately procession — not galloping wildly, but walking with great pride and presence.

- **Satisfaction**: The warm, happy feeling you get when something has gone exactly right — when you have done something well or finished something you set out to do. Musa looked at his dusty red feet with satisfaction after his run.

- **Deliberate**: Done on purpose, carefully and intentionally, not by accident. When Kaka placed the broken pieces of the calabash on the table, she set them down deliberately — with care, not carelessly.

- **Replicate**: To make an exact copy of something, or to repeat something in exactly the same way. Musa thought of Kaka’s soup as something nobody else could replicate — nobody else could make it taste the same way.

- **Ancient**: Very, very old — so old that it goes back to a time long before the people alive today were born. The kakaki trumpets have an ancient sound, meaning they come from a very old tradition that has been kept alive for hundreds of years.

- **Magnificent**: Impressively beautiful and grand in a way that makes you stop and catch your breath. The Durbar was magnificent — not just pretty, but so full of colour and sound and movement that it was hard to take it all in at once.

- **Confession**: When you tell the truth about something wrong you did, especially to someone who has authority or who trusts you. When Musa told Kaka everything about the calabash, that was his confession. The stone left his tummy after his confession.
