## Part One: The Spark of the Lie

***

### Chapter One: The One-Mile Walk

She heard her father’s truck before she saw the school.

The sound came first — a kpa-kpa-kpa that had been the percussion of every morning of Amaka’s life for sixteen years, a mechanical complaint so familiar it had ceased to register at home. In the narrow lanes of their neighbourhood off the Agbani Road, where everyone’s okada backfired and everyone’s generator wheezed, the Peugeot 504 pickup was just another voice in the chorus. Nobody turned. Nobody looked.

But the avenue leading to Hilltop Academy was not Agbani Road.

The avenue was wide enough for three cars and lined on both sides with the kind of trees that had been planted with a plan in mind, their canopies trimmed and identical, the roots contained in neat squares of painted concrete. The families arriving at this hour arrived in the kind of silence that money purchases — the refrigerated hush of SUVs with noise-dampening seals, the gliding opacity of cars that cost more than their compound in Nsukka. Among them, the kpa-kpa-kpa was not background. It was a statement. And the statement it made was one Amaka could not allow to be heard.

“Here, Baba,” she said. “Here is fine.”

Her father, Ezike Okafor, looked at the junction where the commercial road met the beginning of the academy avenue. They were a full mile from the gates. He looked at his daughter, then at the road, measuring the distance with eyes that had spent fifteen years measuring things.

“This is not the school,” he said.

“I know. I’ll walk the last part.” She was already reaching for her bag from behind the seat. “I need the exercise. The sitting is bad for my back.”

He received this explanation with the characteristic patience of a man who trusted his children because trust, for him, was not the reward for suspicion having been satisfied but simply the starting condition. He nodded. He reached across the seat to the bundle of newspaper sitting on the bench between them and held it out.

“In case you get hungry before break.”

The groundnut cake was wrapped in pages from Vanguard and smelled, even through the newspaper, of roasted groundnuts and the particular sweetness of tiger nut oil. It was her mother’s recipe, made in the big pot on Sunday afternoons, the whole compound briefly tasting of something warm and deliberately sweet. At home Amaka would have unwrapped it immediately, eaten half on the way to whatever she was doing. Here she took it with both hands and said thank you and held it carefully, and when her father had turned the truck around and the kpa-kpa-kpa had reduced to a memory behind the commercial traffic, she opened her bag and pushed the bundle to the very bottom.

Beneath the chemistry textbook. Beneath the Agricultural Science folder. In the dark at the bottom where the smell might be absorbed by the fabric lining.

She stood on the pavement and did this, and felt, as she did it, the specific sensation she had been feeling for eight months of Hilltop Academy: a tightness at the back of the throat, as though something had been swallowed too quickly and had not gone all the way down. She pressed the closure of the bag shut. She straightened.

She took the tissue from the pocket of her blazer — she always carried one on Mondays, when the red Nsukka dust from her father’s unpaved road was worst — and crouched at the edge of the pavement and cleaned the toes of her shoes. The shoes were cheap leather, the sort that had aspirations beyond their price point, and she had polished them herself on Sunday night until they held their shine. In a certain light they looked like what they weren’t. She had learned, in eight months, to stay in that certain light.

She dropped the tissue in the gutter. She began to walk.

The mile took seventeen minutes. She knew the time precisely because she timed it every Monday, adjusting her departure from the junction to arrive at the gates with a specific window — not so early that the avenue was quiet and she walked it alone, not so late that the traffic thinned and the car that had dropped her might be noted. She arrived in the middle of the crowd, another body moving with the current, invisible by design.

The gates were iron and high and deliberately so. Everything at Hilltop Academy was designed to announce the size of the distance between what lay inside and what lay outside. The marble entrance courtyard. The hedges trimmed into shapes that served no purpose except to demonstrate that time had been spent on them. The reception desk with its two women in matching polo shirts who smiled at parents with the practised warmth of people who understood that the parents were clients and clients required performance. And the air — the cold, odourless, machine-made air of the building’s climate control system, which hit you at the door with the full weight of its contrast to the Nsukka heat outside.

Amaka walked in. She became, as she became every morning, functionally invisible.

Her grades were first in class, had been since September, and the teachers called on her reliably and received correct answers reliably and moved on. Her uniform was correct. Her shoes were polished. She did nothing that drew attention and nothing that repelled it. She simply existed in the school at a level of visibility that the school, in its particular social architecture, had assigned her and that she had not yet found a way to change.

She went to her locker. She took out what she needed. She walked to the first class of the week, and the day began, and she carried the groundnut cake at the bottom of her bag and said nothing about it to anyone.

### Chapter Two: The Queen Bee’s Court

Somto Adichie had twenty-two thousand followers before she turned fifteen and had not found this remarkable.

She moved through Hilltop Academy with the particular kind of ease that belongs only to people who have never had a reason to practise it. Ease, for Somto, was not an achievement. It was a condition, like height, or good bone structure. She was beautiful with the specific beauty that wealth assists — not by manufacturing it but by removing every obstacle in its path, the way a skilled gardener does not make a flower grow but removes everything that would prevent it from growing into its fullest shape. The right products, administered since childhood. The right clothes, selected with an understanding of what they were communicating. The right phone, in the right case, photographed in the right light.

She sat at the best table in the dining hall as though the table had chosen her, which in the relevant sense was what had happened.

Around her on any given lunchtime: Temi, whose father sat in the Senate and who had inherited from him an instinct for political positioning that expressed itself, in a sixteen-year-old girl, as an extraordinary ability to always be standing close to whoever was currently important. Bintu, who had modelled for a Lagos clothing brand and who wore this fact as an accessory, inserting it into conversation with the casual frequency of someone applying perfume — not constantly, but regularly enough that you never entirely forgot it was there. And Nora, who had spent three years in London and who deployed her British accent with the precision of a specialist tool, using it in exactly those moments when an opinion required the authority of distance.

Amaka had been watching this table for eight months.

She watched it the way a cartographer watches a territory she has studied from a height and not yet walked through — with the mixed authority of someone who knows the shape of the thing and the humility of someone who does not yet know the ground. She watched it from the other side of the dining hall, where the scholarship students and the day students and the ones who did not quite belong anywhere clustered with the quiet solidarity of people who have accepted their position on the map. She watched Somto tilt her head and laugh — the laugh that was not produced but performed, shaped for its audience, the particular pitch and duration of a girl who knows she is being watched and incorporates this knowledge into every gesture.

She looked down at her own collar.

The fraying was at the inner edge of the left lapel, a place not visible from the front but known to her the way you know all the small failures of the things you depend on. She had sewn it twice. It kept coming loose. The blazer itself was second-hand, bought from a graduating student whose name was written in marker on the inside lining: Chiamaka E. Obi, Form Four, 2022. Amaka had not removed the name because the fabric there was delicate and she was afraid of tearing it further. She wore, every day, the remnant of someone else’s identity inside her collar, and this small, daily fact was the most accurate description she had of her position in this school.

She was brilliant. She knew she was brilliant. She had always been brilliant — in the government secondary in her neighbourhood, where brilliance was noticed and celebrated, where teachers said her name with a particular satisfaction and her classmates copied her chemistry notes and she moved through school with the confidence of someone whose specific value has been confirmed repeatedly. Then the Hilltop scholarship exam, which she had sat on a Saturday morning in a primary school hall, working through the mathematics and the comprehension and the abstract reasoning with the focused calm of someone meeting a problem in her language. She had passed with a score the scholarship committee later told her was the highest in six years.

She had arrived at Hilltop in September expecting to be seen.

The school had looked at her grades and put them in a register and moved on. Excellence here was baseline. Every student in this building had been excellent somewhere else; that was how they had arrived. But only some of them were visible, and visibility was determined by a currency that had nothing to do with chemistry or mathematics or the ability to answer every question correctly. Amaka had been here for eight months and had not cracked this currency, had not found its denominations or its exchange rates, and some mornings — like this morning, sitting in the freezing classroom with the correct answer written in her exercise book and the teacher already moving to the next question — she thought she might be invisible forever.

She looked at the table across the dining hall.

Somto was holding court, telling a story with both hands, the jewellery on her wrists catching the light. The table leaned toward her. The table always leaned toward Somto.

Amaka put her food in her mouth and chewed and swallowed and looked at her phone, which held two hundred and fourteen followers and nothing that the world considered interesting, and said nothing.

### Chapter Three: The First Filter

She went home the long way on Wednesday.

This was a habit she had developed without naming it — on the days the afternoon felt particularly heavy, on the days the weight of the double visibility (being seen getting every answer right, being unseen at every other moment) became a kind of physical fatigue, she would walk west instead of east at the school junction, looping through the Government Reserved Area before turning back toward Agbani Road. The GRA was a different city. The roads were paved. The drains functioned. The walls were high and cream-coloured with the kind of finish that was reapplied regularly, without waiting for it to fail.

She walked slowly, which was unlike her. She walked slowly and looked at the houses over the walls and felt, not for the first time and not with any diminishing effect, the particular sensation of standing on the wrong side of a window looking through glass at a lit and heated room.

The white mansion was on the corner of two wide, quiet streets.

It was enormous in the way that certain Nigerian houses are enormous — not vertically but in their horizontal occupation of the earth, as if the owner had decided that breadth was the truest expression of having arrived. The white paint was very fresh. She could smell it faintly from the pavement, a clean chemical smell, newness still outgassing. The compound wall was three metres at least, topped with electric fencing whose insulated wire caught the late afternoon light in small metallic glints. The gate was wrought iron, black, the kind of gate that has been custom-made to communicate something specific about the person who commissioned it. Through the bars, the driveway curved, and at the end of the curve, under a canopy whose aluminium roof was still visibly unchipped, sat a Range Rover Sport.

Midnight blue. Current model. The interior, glimpsed through the window, pale leather.

Amaka stopped walking.

She stood on the pavement outside the gate with her bag on her shoulder and the afternoon heat pressing down and the sound of the estate's ambient silence — generators running somewhere, a lawn mower two streets over — all around her. She looked at the house. She looked at the car. She looked at the gate, its elegant wrought iron, the way it was large enough to stand in front of and still leave the gate clearly visible behind you.

She did not make a decision.

This is the important thing, the thing she would try to explain to herself later in the long nights that followed: she did not decide. The impulse came from somewhere below the level at which decisions are made, from some part of her that was sixteen years old and had been invisible for eight months and that had spent every lunch period watching a table it was not allowed to sit at. The phone was in her hand. The camera was open. She stepped back so the gate filled the frame on her left, the mansion in the soft focus behind her. She looked at the screen. She looked at her face in the screen: the sweat of the walk, the flat light, the ordinary face she had always had.

She opened the filter menu.

The filter did not lie, exactly. It did what filters do: it found the best version of the available light, smoothed what the heat had done to her skin, warmed the white of the mansion to something more golden, and made the whole composition look less like a Wednesday afternoon in Nsukka and more like the curated memory of something. She adjusted the angle. She pressed the shutter.

She looked at the picture.

In the picture, she was the girl who lived here.

She typed the caption without pausing, because pausing would have introduced the part of her brain that knew what she was doing, and that part she did not want.

Daddy’s new toy. Home sweet home. 🏠🔥

She pressed post.

She put the phone in her bag and walked two steps and then the first notification arrived and she stopped. She took the phone out. Seven likes. Then fourteen. Then the numbers stopped being numbers she could track individually and became a stream, a continuous increment, names she didn’t know, comments from strangers, and then — among the strangers, clearly and precisely, a name she did know:

Somto_Official: 🔥👑

The rush was physical. It started in the chest and moved outward through the limbs in a hot wave, a warmth that had nothing to do with the afternoon temperature and everything to do with the brain's ancient, animal response to being looked at by the right pair of eyes. Two characters. A fire emoji and a crown. That was all. But those two characters hit her sternum with the force of something structural, something load-bearing, as if they had arrived and slotted into a space that had been waiting, without her knowledge, to receive exactly this.

She stood on the pavement outside the estate gate with the notifications arriving in a continuous pulse and felt two things simultaneously and felt them with equal intensity, which is the particular cruelty of the first lie: the rush, which was real; and beneath the rush, already there, already settled, already permanent — a stone.

Cold. Dense. Located precisely behind the belly button.

She looked at the post. She looked at the picture. She could delete it. She could delete it right now and it would be gone and none of the numbers would matter and the stone would still be there but the rush would go too, and the crown emoji would go, and the fire emoji would go, and Somto’s attention — that clean, bright, unprecedented quantity of attention — would go with it.

She did not delete it.

She put the phone in her pocket and walked home, and the notifications pulsed against her hip the whole way, each one a small hot pulse like a second heartbeat moving in without permission, and the stone did not move at all.

## Part Two: Building the Glass Castle

### Chapter Four: The Equestrian Hands

She had not expected Thursday.

Thursday arrived with the particular quality of a day that does not announce what it is going to do to you. The morning was ordinary. She rode in her father’s truck to the junction, she walked the mile, she polished her shoes at the pavement edge and dropped the tissue in the gutter. She went through the gates. She became invisible.

Except that she was not invisible.

She knew the moment she turned into the main corridor, because the quality of the air around her had changed in a way that had nothing to do with the air conditioning. Three separate people looked at her as she walked past the first classroom door. Not the passing, automatic look that people give to movement — the look that says: something has moved here and my visual cortex has processed it and moved on. These were the other kind. The looks that land and stay for a half-second too long. The looks that say: I have made a decision about this person and the decision is that they are worth looking at.

She understood before she reached her locker.

At the water fountain outside the Form Four corridor, Somto was standing with Temi and Nora. This was where Somto held her morning court — the fifteen minutes between arrival and the first bell, when the corridor traffic was highest and the standing was most visible. Amaka had walked past this fountain every morning for eight months at precisely the correct speed: fast enough to not appear to be looking, slow enough to see.

Somto was looking directly at her.

“Amaka.”

She stopped. The word landed with its full weight. Somto had never said her name before. Not once. Amaka was aware of this the way you’re aware of things you have been tracking without admitting to yourself that you were tracking them.

“Sit with us at lunch,” Somto said.

It was not phrased as an invitation. It was the announcement of a decision that had already been made and to which Amaka’s agreement was a formality. She accepted it as such.

“Okay,” she said.

The lunch table was smaller from inside than it had appeared from across the dining hall. This was a thing Amaka would learn over the weeks that followed: most things are smaller from inside than they appear from outside, which is both reassuring and faintly disappointing. The girls were funny, sometimes. Somto’s humour was surgical, precise, mostly deployed against people who were not present and therefore safe. Temi laughed at everything, which made her ideal company. Nora issued opinions on things she had experienced in London — restaurants, neighbourhoods, the particular quality of the cold there — and these were received with the deference owed to someone who has been somewhere you haven’t.

Amaka ate carefully and answered questions carefully and felt the structure going up around her with each answer: here is the girl who lives in the GRA estate, here is the girl with the Range Rover father, here is the person that the post suggested and that you are now confirming in real time. She felt it with the specific unease of someone constructing something out of material she doesn’t trust, feeling each addition and wondering about the thing’s total weight.

Chidi arrived late, dropping his tray across from Amaka with the unselfconscious confidence of a boy who is tall and privately educated and has not yet been taught to be careful with his own attention. He looked at Amaka. He looked at her hands on the table.

She saw his eyes go to them.

Her hands were resting beside her tray. They were her hands: the calluses at the base of the right index and middle fingers, the roughened interior of the palm where the hoe handle had worked its own specific geography into her skin over two seasons of helping on the farm. Her father’s yam mounds, on the Saturdays and school holidays when she went with him, the iron hoe heavy in the grip, the red earth giving and resisting in alternation. These were those mornings, written permanently into the skin.

Chidi opened his mouth.

The anxiety arrived in the throat as a sudden, dry absence, as though the saliva had received a memo and evacuated. Her eyes wanted to move — to the tray, to the window, anywhere but his face — and she held them where they were with an effort she hoped didn’t show.

“Your hands,” he said. “They look like... I wasn’t expecting that.”

She heard her own voice produce the word before she had chosen it.

“Equestrian.”

He looked up from her hands to her face.

“I ride,” she said. “Every weekend. My father keeps horses on the estate — I’ve done it since I was eight. The reins do that, if you go hard enough. The leather.”

She watched the word ‘estate’ land. She watched Chidi receive it, cross-reference it against the post he had presumably seen, and nod with the slow, settled nod of a person whose existing picture of someone has been confirmed rather than disrupted.

“Ahh,” he said. “That makes sense actually.”

It did not make sense. It made the opposite of sense. But sense is what we construct from the materials we are given, and the materials she had given him fit together and so he constructed sense from them, and now she had said it, and it existed, and it was one more brick in the structure, and the structure was now taller than she had planned and had no foundation she could point to.

The lie tasted the way she had feared it would: sour milk at the back of the throat. A wrong temperature. Something consumed that would require processing in a way that ordinary food didn’t.

She smiled. She ate. She made a note in the part of her memory reserved for essential information: horses, since eight, estate, reins, leather calluses. She added it to the list.

The list was already longer than she had intended.

### Chapter Five: The Starving Queen

The double life had a schedule, the way all things requiring maintenance have schedules.

From seven to three, she was the girl with the estate. She spoke carefully and consistently. She had begun, in the first week, to research the things she implied — the breeds of horses most commonly kept by wealthy Nigerians, the names of the neighbourhoods in the Nsukka GRA where a house of that size would plausibly sit, the model year of the Range Rover in the picture, what that year’s Range Rover cost. She committed these details to memory with the same focused intensity she brought to chemistry problems, which was significant. She was using her best brain on her worst project.

From three to seven, she was herself.

The forty-five-minute walk home. The unpaved road. The three-room house whose ceiling was unfished concrete in two rooms and bare zinc in the third, so that the rain, when it came, was not a background sound but a direct and personal communication from the sky above. Her mother’s voice from the kitchen, asking how school was, and Amaka giving the true facts and withholding the true atmosphere, because the atmosphere would require context that the context would require explanation.

On the Friday of the second week, her mother came home from the market with a dress.

It was cotton, printed in a pattern of yellow and deep green and small repeating diamonds that had a folk energy, a craft quality, the look of something made near rather than far. Her mother held it against herself, then held it toward Amaka, with the expression she had when she had found something she was genuinely pleased about: the slight brightness around the eyes, the already-settled look of someone who has done the right thing and is not yet aware that someone else is about to contest this.

“For Sunday service,” she said. “I got it from Aunty Nkechi’s cousin. The material is good quality. Feel it.”

Amaka looked at the dress.

She thought of Nora’s blouses. She thought of Somto’s fabric, which had no country and no market stall, which had been selected in a boutique by someone whose job was to select such things. She thought of what it would mean to wear this dress in any configuration of her current life, real or constructed.

“It looks local,” she said.

She said it the way you say a thing that has arrived at the surface before you had a chance to redirect it. The words came out flat and clear and completely without ambiguity, and she heard them in the air between herself and her mother, and the heat arrived immediately: not the hot rush of the notifications but a lower, crawling heat, starting at the base of the neck and moving upward, the specific thermal signature of having done something that cannot be taken back.

Her mother’s face did not change in the ways faces change in dramatic moments. It changed in the smaller, more permanent ways. The small brightness around the eyes went somewhere else. Not quickly. Quietly.

“Sorry, Mama —”

But the pride that had produced the first sentence now closed the apology off with the same automatic reflex, the way a door on a spring closes before you can put your foot in it. The apology dissolved somewhere between the intention and the air. Her mother folded the dress over her arm with the care of someone handling something that still has value, and walked into the bedroom without speaking.

Amaka sat at the kitchen table.

Dinner that night was garri and egusi soup without meat. Her mother served it without comment, set the bowl in the centre where all three of them could reach, and sat. Her father said the grace in the brief, routine way he said it on weekday evenings when they were all tired, the prayer that acknowledges God’s provision and moves on. They ate.

Amaka ate quickly. She was hungry in a physical way that had intensified over the past two weeks, her body running extra systems, spending fuel on the constant management of what she had built and was building. She ate and did not taste what she ate. She said goodnight early and went to her room and lay on the mattress with the phone on her chest.

The account had gained four hundred and thirty new followers since Wednesday. The comments were still coming on the original post, discovered by new arrivals following accounts that had shared it. Strangers were tagging their friends. A lifestyle page with sixty thousand followers had reposted the picture in a collage of “Nigerian youth living their best life.”

She was one thousand, two hundred people’s idea of someone living well.

She lay in her room with the blue light of the phone screen washing over her face and the smell of egusi soup still in the air and the distant sound of her parents’ voices from the other room, talking about something ordinary, and she felt, with a clarity that her body seemed to have reached ahead of her mind, the specific loneliness of the lie: that it does not remove you from the life you are living. It simply makes you a stranger to it. You are still in the room. You are still at the table. But the table is not where you are, and the room does not know you, and you sit in it like someone waiting for a performance that has been cancelled and has not been told.

She turned the phone face-down.

The blue light went away.

She stared at the dark ceiling — the bare zinc above her head, the sky one thin sheet of corrugated metal away — and the stone behind her belly button held its position, as stones do, with perfect and patient stillness.

### Chapter Six: The Trap Snaps Shut

Mr. Okeke had the specific quality of a teacher who teaches a subject the world undervalues and knows it and has decided to be magnificent anyway.

Agricultural Science at Hilltop was considered, by the students, to be among the less consequential subjects — present because the curriculum required it, endured because the exam was there, not meaningful in the way that the sciences and economics were meaningful because it did not connect to any future that the students in this building were planning to occupy. They would not farm. Their parents had not farmed. The chain from this classroom to the earth was long enough to have become theoretical.

Mr. Okeke was aware of this. He had been aware of it for nine years. He wrote on the board in large, careful letters.

MODERN ESTATE FARMING VS. TRADITIONAL METHODS: A DOCUMENTARY.

He put the marker down and turned around.

“This is your term project,” he said. “Sixty percent of your final grade. Groups of four. Primary research — which means you will go into the field. You will interview people who work the earth. You will get soil on your shoes. I do not want Wikipedia. I do not want Google Images. I want the actual earth, in the frame, in high resolution, with human beings on it who have dedicated their lives to it.”

He read out the groups.

Amaka heard her name with Somto’s and did not react. She had learned, in the past two weeks at the new table, to manage her face with a precision that was its own form of work. She kept her expression at the level of mild interest. She wrote her name in the project column of her exercise book. She pressed the pen into the page slightly too hard and the letters came out thicker than usual, which was the only external sign of the cold that had arrived the moment she heard the grouping, a cold that had started in her chest and was moving outward in all directions with a quiet, systematic efficiency.

Somto turned her chair.

“Perfect,” she said. Her face was genuinely pleased; she had the look of someone whose plan is working out. “Amaka — your dad’s estate. We come on Saturday. We film the whole thing — the equipment, the scale, the operation. That gives us the contrast segment with the traditional footage Mr. Okeke wants us to get from the village side.” She opened her phone and was already making the calendar entry. “Saturday morning. What time should we arrive?”

The classroom was still going on around them. Mr. Okeke was still talking about deliverables. Chidi, who had been included in the group, was nodding. Temi was already asking about cameras.

They were all looking at Amaka.

The throat was completely dry. Not the manageable dryness of anxiety, the scratchy feeling she had become used to, but a specific, total absence, as if the tissue itself had been replaced with something that did not produce moisture. She could feel her own swallowing, the futile muscular effort of a mechanism trying to perform a function without the necessary raw material.

She thought, at that moment, of every brick she had laid. The post. The equestrian hands. The horses since eight. The estate. The Range Rover father. Each one placed carefully, each one connected to the ones below it, each one adding height and therefore adding the distance that a fall from this height would cover.

She nodded.

“Nine o’clock,” she said. She was surprised by her voice: level, normal, entirely betraying nothing. “I’ll text you the road.”

“Perfect,” Somto said again.

The trap did not close with a sound. It closed with a silence — the specific silence of a thing that has been building for weeks and has now arrived at its natural conclusion, which has been its destination all along. Amaka wrote the date in her exercise book beneath her name and pressed the pen down again, and the letters were thick again, and Mr. Okeke continued teaching, and the earth he was describing, out there beyond the windows and the climate control, continued being exactly what it always had been: patient, indifferent, and entirely honest.

## Part Three: The Collision of Worlds

### Chapter Seven: The Desperate Blueprint

By Thursday she had a plan. The plan was bad, and she knew it was bad, and this knowing did not prevent her from pursuing it.

She had spent Wednesday night on her back in the dark, running scenarios. The scenarios all terminated in the same place: Saturday morning, red-dirt road, Somto’s face processing the compound, the blue-painted zinc gate, the yam mounds stretching behind the unfinished block house. She had run the scenario at speed, at slow speed, from different angles, and it always ended the same way: the face, and then the withdrawal of everything the face had previously offered, and then the return to invisibility, but a worse invisibility than before — not the neutral invisibility of the first eight months but the active, noted invisibility of someone who has been found out.

Thursday she went to the school bathroom on the ground floor that was sometimes left unlocked. She sat on the closed lid and opened her contacts.

Uncle Emeka was her mother’s older brother. He lived in Enugu, in a house in the GRA that she had visited twice — once for his second daughter’s naming ceremony, once for Christmas, years ago. She remembered the compound: the wall, the gate, the general scale of the thing, the quality of the paint on the house. Not the mansion on the corner. But close enough, in the category of things that could, from a camera angle, be mistaken for it.

She called him.

Her thumb on the call button was the last moment before consequence, the last clean moment of the day, and she pressed it and heard it ring and committed.

Uncle Emeka answered on the fourth ring, his voice carrying the slightly elevated quality of a man who receives calls throughout the day and has developed a professional neutrality toward them.

“Amaka. How are you, my daughter?”

“I’m fine, Uncle. I’m well. I’m calling because I have a school project.”

She heard herself construct it as she spoke it, which was the most frightening thing about the call: she was not reciting something she had prepared but building it in real time, each sentence arriving slightly ahead of the one she was currently saying, the architecture going up as she walked into it. A documentary project. Agricultural science. The specific requirements of the assignment. And then — the bridge to what she needed: the group needed to film at a large working estate, and she had told them her family had one, and could she bring her friends to film at his house for just a few hours on Saturday morning, and she would explain everything to her parents after, it was just for the school project, she didn’t want to bother them.

Uncle Emeka asked questions.

He asked them in the reasonable, probing way of a man who has raised children and recognises the sound of something left out of an explanation. He asked about her parents. He said he would need to check with Aunty Ngozi. He said he would call back.

She sat in the bathroom with the phone in her hands and the cold tile under her feet and the sound of the school going about its business outside the locked door, and she felt, precisely, the specific sensation of a structure swaying.

She came home that evening and stood in the dark hallway of her father’s house with its one bulb on a wire and the crack in the wall that had been there since before she was born, and she called him again. Uncle Emeka. Amending, filling in, adding the detail the morning call had missed, the structure getting taller with each addition and the foundation not changing, remaining what it was, which was nothing.

She was speaking quietly. She had turned slightly toward the wall, the unconscious positioning of someone who does not want to be heard, and she was building the story with the rapid, focused energy of someone who has passed the point of caution and is now fully committed to the engineering problem.

She did not see the light from the kitchen doorway change.

She did not hear the slippers on the cement floor.

### Chapter Eight: The Heavy Silence

She ended the call.

She turned around.

Her father was standing in the kitchen doorway.

Not for a moment. Not having just arrived, drawn by the sound of her voice. He had been there for some time — she could tell by the quality of his stillness, which was the stillness of someone who has already received the full content of what they came to hear and has moved past receiving into something slower and more internal. He was not looking at her. He was looking at his hands.

His hands were at his sides, and he was looking at them the way you look at tools you have relied on for a very long time when a question has been raised about what, exactly, they have built. The red Nsukka laterite was in the creases of his knuckles, in the lines of his palms, in the half-moons beneath his nails — a stain that no washing fully removed, that had been there so long it was less a mark on the skin than a quality of the skin itself. Fifteen years of it. The yam mounds in all weathers, the cassava, the pepper, the seasonal rotation that he planned each year with the care of someone managing a portfolio in which the stakes are not money but food and school fees and the sustained possibility of the next generation.

He did not raise his voice.

This was the thing she had not prepared for. She had prepared for several versions of this — the belt, which he had not used since she was nine; the raised volume, which she could argue against; the theatrical disappointment, which she could weather. She had not prepared for the version that arrived, which was the quiet voice at the end of a long day, stripped of everything except the truth of a thing.

“I break my back in the sun,” he said, to his hands. “Every day. So that you can sit in an air-conditioned classroom with children who did not earn their place with their brains. Children whose fathers bought their seats. You earned yours.”

He paused. The word ‘earned’ sat in the hallway between them.

“I eat less than I should, to make the fees. I wear clothes I am not proud of. I fix that truck with whatever the mechanic says can be fixed this month and leave the rest for next month, because this month the fees are more important than the exhaust. I have done this for eight months without complaint and I will do it for as long as you need me to, because you are my daughter and you earned that place and I will not have you lose it.”

He looked up.

She met his eyes and immediately looked away, which was cowardly and she knew it was cowardly.

“My sweat feeds you,” he said. “My sweat sends you to school. My sweat is the reason you have the option to be ashamed of me. And you have chosen to use that option.”

The cold that arrived was not the stone behind the belly button, which she had carried for weeks and had become accustomed to. This was different. This started in the chest, behind the sternum, and it moved outward and downward with a slow, unstoppable progression until it reached the soles of her feet, and for a moment she did not feel the floor.

She sat down. Her legs made the decision without consulting her. She was sitting on the small wooden stool in the hallway, her bag still on her shoulder, looking at the floor.

“You have sold your family’s dignity,” he said, “for the applause of strangers. Strangers who will not be in this house when the rain comes. Strangers who will not sit at this table. Strangers who will never know your mother’s cooking or what she gave up so you could have new textbooks. Strangers for whom you have made your own father into something to be dropped off at a distance.”

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the evening was moving through the compound. The breadfruit tree shadow was long on the red earth.

“I will not help you borrow another man’s house to show to people who think they know you,” he said. “If they are your friends, let them come here. This house. This road, with the dust on it. Let them see the yam mounds. Let them see the hoe leaning against the fence. Let them sit at this table. If they cannot sit at this table and eat what is on it, then they are not your friends. They are your audience. And you have been performing for an audience that does not know your name.”

He walked back into the kitchen.

She heard the water running. She heard him wash his hands — the specific rhythm of it, something she had heard ten thousand times without registering, the water and the soap and the water again, the thorough, particular way he washed them. She heard the tap close. She heard the towel on the hook.

Then silence, the ordinary silence of a man who has said what needed saying and returned to the tasks of the evening.

She sat on the stool for a long time.

The phone was in her pocket. Its weight had always been there, a reliable pressure against her hip, but it felt different now. Not lighter. Heavier in a different way, the way objects feel heavy when you have been reminded of what they cost.

### Chapter Nine: The Long Night

She did not sleep.

She lay on the mattress in the dark and the ceiling was close above her and the zinc ticked as the night cooled it, each tick a small metallic note in the silence, the sound of a house releasing the heat of the day with the unhurried thoroughness of something that has been doing this every night for years.

She made lists.

Option one: illness. Genuine or performed, it didn’t matter — a call to Somto Saturday morning, voice scratchy, I can’t make it, I’m so sorry, we’ll reschedule. Problem: Mr. Okeke had said primary research was non-negotiable and group attendance was mandatory and an absence would require a medical certificate and the project would be docked, and her scholarship was conditional on her grades and she could not afford a docked project, not this project, not sixty percent.

Option two: another location. Find somewhere, anywhere, that could stand in for what she had implied. Uncle Emeka was no longer available — her father had made that impossible. She did not know anyone else with the kind of property the lie had described. She could not fabricate a location from nothing. She had no material.

Option three: tell them.

She lay with option three for a long time. She tested its weight. She imagined the shape of it — the actual sentences, the specific words, Somto’s face receiving those words, the rearrangement of Somto’s expression from the current version, which was warm and proprietorial, to the other version, the one behind the warmth, the calculation.

She picked up her phone.

The app was there, its familiar icon. She opened it. The post was still there, sitting in the feed with its one thousand, four hundred and something likes, the comments still arriving from the outer edges of the internet where things travel slowly. Her new followers were mostly quiet now, having subscribed to a version of her that had not yet posted again and were waiting with the patient credulity of people who have bought tickets to a show.

She pressed and held the post.

The delete option appeared.

She pressed it.

The post was gone.

The numbers were gone. The comments. The fire emojis. The crown. Somto’s two-character validation and the four hundred strangers who had reshared it and the lifestyle page with sixty thousand followers. All of it retracted in a single gesture, as if it had never existed.

She put the phone down and lay in the silence and the silence was absolute and she thought: this is what I should have done on Wednesday on that pavement outside the white gate.

She picked the phone back up. She opened the App Store. She reinstalled Instagram. The account restored immediately. The followers were still there. The post was gone but the accumulation of attention it had caused — the architecture of the new visibility, the table, Somto’s attention, Chidi’s, all of it constructed on the original post — was still there because it lived in the real world and not in the app.

She put the phone down again.

This is the thing about the lie at this stage: it is no longer about the original action. The original post could be deleted; the original post was gone. But the lie had moved off the screen and into the world, had grown legs in the world, was walking around in the world in the form of a group project and a Saturday visit and a film camera and sixty percent of a grade. Deleting the post was like taking down the sign of a business that is already operating; the customers were already inside.

She got up at some point before two and sat at the small desk with the exercise book open in front of her. She held the pen. She wrote nothing. She went back to the mattress and lay with her arm over her eyes and made no further progress on any solution and the night continued on its own schedule, indifferent to hers.

At five, the roosters.

At five-fifteen, the sound of her father in the kitchen.

She lay and listened to him move through the morning — the gas catching, the water, the particular sound of the enamel pot on the gas ring, the quiet of a man who knows this kitchen well enough to navigate it in the first dark of the morning without adjusting anything. She lay and listened and felt something that was neither hope nor resolution but was perhaps the earlier thing, the thing that comes before those things: the exhausted, reluctant willingness to stop running, not because she had found a direction but because running in all directions simultaneously had finally spent itself.

She did not know, yet, what she would do on Saturday morning.

But she knew what she would not do.

She would not borrow a house. She would not fake an illness. She would not perform, one more time, for an audience that did not know her name.

She lay in the almost-dark with the roosters and the gas ring and her father’s slippers on the cement floor, and the zinc above her ticked its last few ticks of the night, and the sky outside the window moved from black to the dark blue of almost-morning, and she was still who she had always been, even if who she had always been was terrified of Saturday.

## Part Four: The Rain and the Root

***

### Chapter Ten: The Tinted Windows

Saturday morning arrived with the specific clarity of a day that has decided to be itself.

The sky over Nsukka was the deep, washed blue of the harmattan’s departure — not the soft blue of the middle season but a high, scrubbed blue, the blue of a sky that has recently been cleaned by something large and is now showing the result. The air carried in it the mineral smell of rain that was somewhere over the hills to the west, building its argument, not yet ready to deliver it.

The compound looked, in this light, exactly like what it was.

The unfinished block house — three courses of block still exposed on the eastern wall where the plaster had been budgeted for a later year. The zinc roof, honest and functional, no pretension to other materials. The blue-painted gate, its blue the faded, layered blue of something painted once and then again and then allowed to develop its own patina of dust and weather. And behind the house, extending from the back wall to the compound fence, the yam mounds: long parallel rows of raised earth, each mound precise and rounded, the seedlings at their crests green and delicate and receiving the morning sun at a specific angle that made each one briefly luminous.

Amaka was sitting on the wooden bench outside the front door.

She was not looking at her phone. Her phone was in her blazer pocket but she was not looking at it, which was perhaps the most significant thing about that Saturday morning — that she sat in the compound of her father’s house and did not reach for the screen that usually mediated between her and whatever she was feeling. She sat and felt what she was feeling directly, which was: terror, which was unpleasant; and beneath the terror, something older and quieter that she did not have a word for yet but that was connected, she thought, to the sound of her father in the kitchen at five in the morning, navigating the dark with the confidence of someone who knows where everything is.

She heard it before she saw it.

An engine, but not the kind she knew from this road — not the okadas and the buses and her father’s truck. A different kind of engine, smooth and well-fed, making the minimum sound required for a vehicle of its size to move forward. The hydraulic compression of quality suspension absorbing the red-dirt road’s imperfections with the quiet efficiency of expensive engineering.

The matte-black SUV came around the bend.

It was enormous. It occupied the road the way the road had always secretly been designed for a vehicle exactly this size and had simply been waiting. It stopped in front of the compound gate without the driver appearing to apply any particular effort.

The doors opened.

Somto stepped out first, already assessing, her camera bag over one shoulder, her sunglasses doing their work. Temi behind her, phone raised, filming the street in an establishing-shot kind of way. Chidi last, dropping from the high step with the casual physical confidence of someone who has spent years in vehicles like this one. They looked at the road. They looked at the compound gate. They looked at the yam mounds visible over the top of the fence. They looked at each other.

Amaka stood up.

She crossed the compound. She opened the gate. She looked at them standing there on the red-dirt road with their equipment and their clean clothes and their faces arranged in the specific expression of people who have arrived somewhere that is not what they were expecting and have not yet decided what to do about this.

“This is my home,” she said.

She said it in the level voice she had been practising, without preparation, since some time before dawn. Not an apology. Not an explanation. A statement of a fact, offered to people who were now in possession of it.

The road was quiet around them. The air smelled of earth and the approaching rain and the distant cook-fire of someone’s morning from two compounds away.

Somto’s face was doing the thing it did when it was processing something unscripted — the quick internal navigation, the moment before the managed expression arrived. In the gap before it arrived, Amaka saw something she had not expected to see there: not mockery; not the preparation of a dismissal. Just a girl, sixteen years old, standing in a red-dirt road in Nsukka, looking at something she had not encountered before, and not yet having a category for it.

Neither of them spoke. The sky above the compound fence had changed colour since Amaka last looked at it. The blue had darkened by several degrees, and the wind that came off the hills to the west had shifted in a way that carried information for people who had lived in this town long enough to read it.

Amaka looked up.

The sky was making a decision.

### Chapter Eleven: The Gold in the Mud

The rain on Nsukka did not arrive the way rain arrived in other places.

It did not approach. It descended. The sky, which had been considering the matter for some hours, arrived at its conclusion all at once, and the conclusion was water, in the full quantity that six months of stored cloud could provide, delivered from a considerable height with no particular concern for what was beneath.

The compound became a river in forty seconds.

The red earth went dark immediately, the surface tension of the dry soil giving way to the saturation of the instant, and the water found the paths of least resistance and moved along them with the purposeful efficiency of water that has been here before and knows the routes. The yam mounds began to shed. The carefully constructed sides, each one shaped by her father’s hands over hours, began releasing their material into the rising flow at the base of the rows.

Then her father’s voice came from the back of the compound.

Not a shout of panic — she had never heard her father panic, which was a fact she had not thought about before but thought about now, in the moment of hearing the voice. It was the specific high-carrying urgency of a man who has identified a problem and knows precisely what it requires.

“Amaka! The drainage ditch — the eastern end! Come!”

She knew immediately. The drainage ditch ran along the eastern wall, dug by her father in March to carry the first heavy rains away from the mound bases. The eastern end had loose soil — he had mentioned it twice, she had been present both times, she had been half-listening both times as she so often half-listened to things that were part of the life she was half-inhabiting. If the eastern end collapsed under a rainfall this sudden and this heavy, the water would pool at the base of the mounds, undercut the structures, carry the season’s seedlings toward the back drain and into the ravine beyond the fence.

A whole season.

She looked at Somto. She looked at her own shoes, which were the good ones, the polished cheap leather she wore to present herself as the version of herself that could sit at the right table. She looked at the mud already spreading across the compound floor, thick and red and entirely indifferent to shoes.

She kicked them off.

Not a decision. The same below-language impulse that had raised the phone at the mansion gate moved her now in the opposite direction with the same quality of bypassing thought: she was already crossing the compound, barefoot, the mud cold and dense between her toes, and the tool lean-to was beside the kitchen door, and the heavy iron hoe was leaning against the inside wall where it always leaned, and her hand found it with the certainty of a hand that has found this handle before.

The weight of it was familiar. The specific balance of iron head and wood handle, the way it wanted to hang, the adjustment in the grip that the weight required. Her muscles knew this. They had known it since she was twelve, learning alongside her father on Saturday mornings, the hoe heavy at first and then less heavy, her body growing into the tool the way bodies grow into the things they are repeatedly asked to do.

She came around the eastern corner of the house and found her father already working, knee-deep in the collapse, his hands and the blade of his own hoe moving in the fast, precise rhythm of someone who has spent fifteen years developing an intimate understanding of this soil’s behaviour under pressure. The drainage ditch had gone in a two-metre section. Water was pooling fast, already reaching the base of the nearest mounds.

She drove the hoe in.

The mud at the collapse had the consistency of something that had not decided whether to be solid or liquid, resisting and yielding in alternation, requiring you to read it with each stroke rather than apply a fixed technique. Her father’s movements and her own fell into coordination without discussion: the unconscious, efficient choreography of two people who have done this together and whose bodies remember the geometry of working in a shared space around a shared problem.

The rain was complete. She was soaked through from the first ten seconds — her hair, her clothes, her face, all of it simply wet, the distinction between wet and dry having become irrelevant. The mud climbed her shins and then her knees. She kept working.

She was not thinking about Somto. She was not thinking about the project or the sixty percent or the table or Instagram or the stone or any of it. She was thinking about the mounds, and the water, and the direction of the flow, and the angle of the hoe in the collapsing earth, and the seedlings at the crests of the rows that were holding because two people were in the mud making them hold.

Time had a different quality in it, the way time always has a different quality when the body is fully occupied with a necessary thing. She did not know how long it took. She knew when it was done: the water found the redirected channel and moved along it rather than toward the mounds, the flow that had been chaotic became ordered, the pooling at the base of the rows stopped climbing. Her father straightened. He looked at what they had done.

He looked at her.

She was standing in the mud with the hoe across her hands and the rain still coming down and her clothes ruined and her feet submerged and her chest working hard from the sustained effort, and her father looked at her the way he had looked at the drainage ditch when it was done: taking in the accomplished fact of it, measuring its quality, acknowledging what had been required.

Then she remembered.

She turned.

Somto was standing at the edge of the mound rows. She had not come in — she was at the exact boundary where the flooring of the compound met the soft earth of the farm, her clean shoes at the margin. But she had been there for some time. The camera that had come out of the bag was raised and pointing, and it had been pointing, Amaka understood from the angle and the stillness, since before the drainage ditch was fixed.

It had been pointing for a long time.

It had captured all of it.

### Chapter Twelve: The True Harvest

The rain stopped.

It stopped the way Nsukka rain stops — categorically, the sky transitioning from the full statement of the downpour to a dripping quiet and then a clean, mineral silence in the time it takes to draw three slow breaths. The compound steamed faintly where the hot earth received the standing water. The sky above the fence was moving from grey through the grey-white of thin cloud toward the specific blue of a Nsukka afternoon that has said what it needed to say.

Amaka stood in the mud.

She was covered in it from the knee down, and her clothes were wet through and dark with it at the hem and the elbows, and her hair was plastered flat, and she was holding the hoe that she had held in this same field since she was twelve years old learning the angle, and she was looking at Somto across the mud and the mound rows and the clean, standing air between them.

She waited.

She had been waiting, in a sense, for three weeks — since the moment she had posted the picture of the mansion gate, she had been in the process of arriving here, at this moment, in this mud, with this face across from her, and now the moment was here and she was in it and she looked at Somto and waited for the verdict.

Somto lowered the camera.

She was not laughing. Amaka had prepared for the laughter and it was not there. The expression on Somto’s face was the open one — not the managed warmth of the table, not the performed ease, not the social face that she wore as reliably as she wore her jewellery. The unmanaged expression, the one that arrived before the managed one had time to get there. Amaka had only seen it once before, in the unguarded half-second at the compound gate when the SUV doors first opened and the expectation met the reality.

Somto looked at the yam mounds. She looked at the drainage ditch, the redirected water moving orderly along it toward the street drain. She looked at Amaka’s father, who had moved to the row of nearest seedlings and was examining each one with the calm, systematic attention of a man assessing what the water had taken and what it had left. She looked at Amaka.

“I’ve never seen anyone work like that,” she said.

She said it quietly, the way you say something that has arrived in you without your having sent for it, and that you are reporting honestly before the rest of yourself can apply the usual processing.

“My dad signs papers,” she said. “There are people, always. People to manage things, people to handle things, people to deal with whatever it is. He looks at reports afterward and asks questions and approves things.” She looked at the mounds, the specific work visible in the earth, the mounds that were standing because two people had been in the mud at the right moment. “You actually keep things alive.”

Amaka set the hoe against the fence post.

She looked at her hands. The mud covered the calluses, but they were there, beneath it — the real thing, the earned thing, the specific record of the Saturdays and the school holidays and the iron hoe and the red Nsukka earth. Not equestrian. Never equestrian. Something older and more direct than that.

“The post was a lie,” she said.

She said it to the yam mounds first and then raised her eyes to Somto’s. The sentence sat between them in the clean, rain-washed air. It was not a confession in the dramatic sense — no tremble in the voice, no elaborate build-up. Just the fact, offered plainly, the way her father offered facts.

Somto absorbed it.

“I know,” she said.

Amaka looked at her.

“Not from the beginning. But Nora thought the filter was very heavy. And you talked about horses the way someone talks about something they’ve researched.” She paused. “Not the way someone talks about something they’ve done.”

“Then why —”

“Because you’re the best student in our year and you’d been invisible for eight months and the post made you interesting enough to look at.” The honesty in this was the kind that costs something to say, and Somto looked as though she was aware of the cost. “What was interesting was already there. I just needed a reason to look.”

Amaka’s father came around the corner of the house with the mud-cloth in his hands, cleaning the blade of his hoe with the automatic, thorough movements of a man who puts tools away properly. He looked at the group standing in his compound with the unhurried appraisal he applied to everything: taking in the fact of them, measuring what they needed, making no drama of it.

“The mounds are holding,” he said, to Amaka. Then he looked at the group. “You are welcome to eat before you go.”

He said it simply and turned back toward the kitchen, and after a moment the gas ring ignited and the ordinary, domestic sounds of food preparation came through the window.

Temi looked at Chidi. Chidi looked at the mounds, and then at Amaka’s mud-covered legs, and then at the compound with a directness that was different from the directness he’d brought to the dining table — less assessed, more present.

“I’d like to eat,” Somto said. She said it to Amaka. “If that’s all right.”

They ate at the table in the compound, the four of them and Amaka’s father and the sound of Amaka’s mother arriving home from Saturday market with her basket, and the food was rice and stew from the pot and the table was what it was and the compound was what it was and nobody performed anything.

Later she would remember Somto eating with her hands because the spoon was not perfectly clean, not with the expression of someone tolerating a condition but with the expression of someone doing the obvious thing. She would remember Chidi asking her father about the rotation schedule for the yams, and her father explaining it with the care he gave to things he knew well. She would remember Temi laughing at something, a real laugh rather than the table laugh, and noticing the difference.

***

The afternoon, after they had gone, was long and clean.

She helped her father wash the mud from the truck. They stood in the compound with the hose and the bucket and worked the panels and the wheel wells in the comfortable silence of people who have done this together before. The water ran red and then pink and then clear. The truck emerged from the process looking, if not new, then honestly itself.

She dried her hands on the cloth. She took her phone out.

The false post was gone — she had deleted it in the dark of Friday night. But the account was there, and the followers were there, and the empty space where the post had been. She looked at the empty space.

Then she turned to her father, who was coiling the hose with the neat, habitual movements of a man who stores things properly.

“Baba,” she said. “Can I photograph your hands?”

He looked at her. He looked at his hands — the hose-water still dripping from them, the red laterite in the creases, the cracked knuckles, the full record of fifteen years of the earth. He had looked at them, twice now in two days, with the searching quality of someone asking them a question. This time whatever he saw in them, he was satisfied with the answer.

He held them out.

She looked through the camera. No filter. The afternoon light was not flattering in the way filters are flattering — it was honest, which meant the light showed what the light showed: the red earth in the creases, the cracked skin, the specific topography of hands that had worked. Between the loosely cupped palms, a single freshly-pulled yam, still wearing its coat of red Nsukka soil, its roots still attached.

She pressed the shutter.

She looked at the picture. It was not beautiful in the curated sense — it would not fit in a lifestyle collage, would not carry a fire emoji by instinct. It was something else. It was the thing itself, without mediation. The wealth that was actually there, in the actual place, in the actual hands that had built it.

She opened the post screen.

She put the picture in.

She typed:

My father’s wealth. The real estate.

She pressed post.

She put the phone in her pocket.

The evening was doing its particular thing to the Nsukka sky — the light going amber and low, catching the remaining drops on the breadfruit tree’s leaves, turning the wet red earth of the compound into something that glowed from within, as if the earth itself were lit. From inside the house she heard her mother setting the table — the specific sounds of it, the bowls, the bench moved on the floor, the ordinary and familiar orchestration of a family returning to a meal. Her father, somewhere behind her, was whistling the low, tuneless whistle he made on the evenings when the work had been good.

She stood in the compound and breathed in.

The air was washed clean by the rain, carrying the mineral richness of the wet earth and the faint sweetness of the yam seedlings and the cook-fire smell from two compounds away and the particular Nsukka evening smell that she had breathed every day of her life and never catalogued because it was simply the air, the ordinary unremarkable air of the place she came from.

She breathed it in. She breathed it all the way down, to the full capacity of her lungs, without restriction, without management, without the dry scratch of the lie in the throat that had been there for three weeks like a stone in a shoe.

Clean. Full. Entirely itself.

She breathed out.

Inside the house, the table was set for three people, and the food was what it was, and the house was what it was, and the compound behind her was drying in the evening light, and the mounds were standing, and the seedlings were holding, and she walked through the door.

She was Amaka Okafor.

She was, precisely and completely, herself.

And for the first time in three weeks, this was enough.
